Science · Cortisol & risk-taking 02 / 13

Eight days of elevated cortisol cut risk-taking by 44%.

A controlled experiment dosed healthy adults with hydrocortisone for 8 days, matching the cortisol elevation seen in real traders during a market downturn. Risk-taking collapsed.
Utility curvature α (lower = more risk-averse)
Certainty equivalent for a £100 coin-flip lottery
What it means. Sustained cortisol reshapes how the brain values uncertain payoffs — the same risky lottery becomes substantially less attractive. Causal: appears under pharmacological dosing in healthy volunteers, not just correlated with trader stress.
Source: Kandasamy et al. 2014, PNAS 111(9):3608–13 · N=36, 8-day weight-adjusted hydrocortisone elevated cortisol 69%; α 0.50→0.35 (P=0.022); CE £25→£14 (−44%) · pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1317908111
Science · PFC and arousal 03 / 13

Too much stress chemistry breaks the prefrontal cortex's deliberative function.

The prefrontal cortex operates within a narrow window of arousal. Push catecholamines (norepinephrine, dopamine) too low or too high and PFC working memory degrades. The "inverted-U" is demonstrated in monkey single-neuron recordings and decades of pharmacological work.
What it means. Acute stress drives catecholamines past the optimal zone. PFC working memory drops. Decisions that need deliberation — multi-step planning, considering counterfactuals, holding future scenarios in mind — degrade. Lower-order circuits (amygdala, striatum) take over. Causal evidence: β-blockers restore PFC function under stress; α2A-agonists (guanfacine) shift tolerance higher.
Arnsten 2009, Nat Rev Neurosci 10:410–22 · nature.com/articles/nrn2648 · Empirical inverted-U in monkey PFC neurons: Vijayraghavan et al. 2007, Nat Neurosci 10:376–84 · nature.com/articles/nn1846 · Lab figure: medicine.yale.edu/lab/arnsten/research/invertedu
Science · Whole-brain network reconfiguration 04 / 13

Acute stress reconfigures the whole brain: salience network up, executive network down.

In a placebo-controlled fMRI study, exposing healthy adults to a fear-related acute stressor caused a brain-wide network shift. The "salience hijack" is mediated by noradrenergic signaling — propranolol blocks it, the cortisol blocker metyrapone does not.
Primary source · open access
"Stress-Related Noradrenergic Activity Prompts Large-Scale Neural Network Reconfiguration"
Hermans, van Marle, Ossewaarde, Henckens et al. · Science 334(6059):1151–53 · 25 Nov 2011
Paper: science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1209603  ·  Open-access PDF (figures): pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2011/hermans2011a.pdf
UP under stress
Salience network — anterior insula, dorsal ACC, amygdala, hypothalamus, midbrain. Functional connectivity strengthens with stress-response magnitude.
DOWN under stress
Executive control network — dorsolateral PFC, lateral parietal cortex. Top-down control weakens.
Noradrenaline drives it
Propranolol BLOCKS the reconfiguration. A β-adrenergic blocker prevents the stress-induced network shift — implicating the locus coeruleus as the proximal cause.
Cortisol does NOT
Metyrapone does NOT block the fast salience shift. Cortisol drives a slower, recovery-phase reconfiguration on a different time-scale.
Science · Time horizon compression 05 / 13

Under acute cortisol, we choose smaller, sooner over larger, later.

A double-blind hydrocortisone challenge: 15 minutes after dosing, subjects became markedly more impulsive in intertemporal choice — preferring small immediate payoffs over larger delayed ones. Acute and time-limited; aligns with non-genomic glucocorticoid signaling on fast timescales.
Acute · 15 min post-cortisol
Strongly increased preference for the small, soon reward over the larger, delayed reward. Effect present at 15 min, absent at 195 min.
Cornelisse et al. 2018, Psychoneuroendocrinology · 10 mg hydrocortisone vs placebo
Chronic · 8-day cortisol elevation
Risk premium collapses 44%; utility curvature flattens.
Kandasamy et al. 2014, PNAS
Mechanism · why horizons matter
Myopic loss aversion. When the evaluation horizon shortens, loss aversion (λ ≈ 2.25) mechanically dominates — explaining the equity premium puzzle and why high-frequency re-evaluation amplifies risk aversion.
Benartzi & Thaler 1995, QJE
Honest caveat
Acute psychosocial-stress effects on discounting are mixed across studies. The cleanest causal evidence is pharmacological (Cornelisse 2018) and chronic cortisol (Kandasamy 2014); psychosocial stressors in the lab show more variable effects.
Sources: Cornelisse et al. 2018, Psychoneuroendocrinology 88:161–68 · Kandasamy et al. 2014, PNAS · Benartzi & Thaler 1995, QJE 110(1):73–92
Science · Defensive-response circuits 06 / 13

Under acute threat, control shifts from deliberation to reflexive defensive circuits.

Four columns of the periaqueductal gray (PAG) — a midbrain structure — each drive a different defensive program. The brain hands off from cognitive appraisal (ventromedial PFC) to PAG-mediated programs as threat proximity rises.

Psychology · Dual-process judgment 07 / 13

Kahneman: two systems — and stress pushes us into the wrong one.

Daniel Kahneman's dual-process framework is the psychological bridge between the neuroscience we just saw and the practical judgment errors analysts and PMs make. When Arnsten's PFC goes offline, System 2 goes offline — and System 1 takes over the decision.
Fast · automatic · effortless
System 1
Pattern-matching, intuition, association. Runs constantly, below awareness. Biases live here: anchoring, availability, loss aversion, narrow framing, WYSIATI ("what you see is all there is").
  • Dominates under load / stress — cognitive resources exhausted, System 2 can't monitor.
  • Fools us into treating recent vivid events (last week's CPI surprise) as representative.
  • Substitutes easy questions — "is this a good trade?" silently becomes "does the story feel right?"
Slow · deliberate · effortful
System 2
Rule-based analysis, mental arithmetic, holding multiple scenarios in mind. Requires an intact PFC — the exact circuit that goes offline under catecholamine surge.
  • Energy-expensive — ego-depletion effects, glucose sensitive.
  • Lazy by default — endorses System 1 unless actively prompted.
  • Outperforms experts when structured (Meehl's simple-rule-beats-clinician finding Kahneman repeatedly cites).
Why this matters for investment. Most PMs believe their decisions are System 2 — models, data, frameworks. In fact, under stress, cognitive load, or time pressure, decisions silently drop into System 1. The neuroscience (Arnsten, Hermans) gives a biological mechanism; Kahneman gives the practical vocabulary for what it feels like and how to design around it.
Kahneman 2011, Thinking, Fast and Slow (Farrar, Straus & Giroux) · publisher page · Concept origins: Stanovich & West 2000; Evans & Stanovich 2013 Perspectives on Psychological Science 8(3):223. Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel in Economics (with Vernon Smith) for prospect theory developed with Amos Tversky.
Psychology · Noise in expert judgment 08 / 13

Kahneman, Noise: the same expert, the same case, a different answer.

Kahneman's 2021 work (with Sibony & Sunstein) reframed the bias problem. Bias is systematic error in one direction. Noise is unwanted variability — the same expert reaching different judgments on identical cases depending on irrelevant factors: mood, time of day, recent stimuli, the room. Organizations systematically underestimate how much of their decision quality is destroyed by noise rather than bias.
Insurance underwriters
~55% median difference in premiums set for identical cases by different underwriters at the same firm. Executives estimated the gap would be ~10%. It was 5×.
Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein 2021, Noise
Judges
Sentences for the same offense vary dramatically with irrelevant factors: lunch timing, weather, whether the judge's home football team won the weekend before.
Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso 2011, PNAS on parole judges
Radiologists
Re-shown the same scans days later, radiologists disagree with themselves ~20% of the time. "Occasion noise" — the same expert is not the same expert on two occasions.
The fix — decision hygiene
Structured decomposition · independent judgments before aggregation · delayed opinion-formation. Cuts variance in expert judgment by 30–50%. Same lever that makes process beat self-awareness (slide 12).
Why this matters for investment. The same analyst's conviction on the same thesis varies day-to-day. The same PM's risk appetite drifts with sleep, headlines, last Friday's P&L. Not bias — noise. It's invisible until you measure it and it destroys judgment quality. Kahneman: "Wherever there is judgment, there is noise — and usually more of it than you think."
Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein 2021, Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment (Little, Brown) · publisher · Kahneman's NYT summary: nytimes.com/2021/05/15/business/noise-daniel-kahneman · Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso 2011 PNAS 108(17):6889 on judge decisions
Psychology · Confidence vs. accuracy 09 / 13

Calibration, not confidence, predicts accuracy.

Extreme confidence is disconnected from being right when it is poorly calibrated — and strongly predictive when it is well calibrated. The literature cuts both ways; what separates signal from noise is the reporter's track record, not the confidence level itself.

Where confidence disconnects from accuracy

  • Dunning-Kruger (1999). Bottom-quartile performers estimate themselves in the 62nd percentile; top performers underestimate. Low skill → high confidence, and vice-versa.
  • Alpert & Raiffa 1982. Subjects' "90% confidence intervals" contained the true value only ~50% of the time. Classic overprecision.
  • Barber & Odean 2001 QJE. Overconfident traders (proxied by male gender) trade 45% more and underperform by 2.65 percentage points annually. More confidence → worse returns.
  • Lichtenstein & Fischhoff 1977. The hard-easy effect: overconfident on difficult tasks, underconfident on easy ones. Both extremes miscalibrate.
  • Kahneman & Klein 2009. "Illusion of validity" — analysts maintain high confidence despite poor track records when feedback is noisy or delayed.
  • Tetlock 2005. Hedgehogs (confident, single-framework) performed near chance on political forecasts. More telegenic, less accurate.

Where confidence does predict accuracy

  • Superforecasters (Tetlock & Gardner 2015). Top 2% of forecasters use granular confidence estimates — "73%" not "likely." When they say 73%, it happens ~73% of the time.
  • Cohen, Polk & Silli 2010 "Best Ideas." Top-conviction picks of actively managed funds outperform broad portfolios by ~6% annualized. Extreme conviction from skilled PMs is a real signal.
  • Cremers & Petajisto 2009 RFS. High-Active-Share funds (concentrated conviction) outperform low-Active-Share ones.
  • Murphy & Winkler weather forecasters. Well-calibrated (when they say 70% chance of rain, it rains ~70% of the time) because feedback is rapid and objective.
  • Sanders, Hangya & Kepecs 2016 Neuron. Under Bayesian signal-detection, accuracy is highest at the confidence tails — for a calibrated reporter. Extreme confidence is a signal only when the reporter is Bayesian.
The practical implication. The question is not "how confident is the analyst?" — it is "what is this analyst's calibration at this level of confidence?" Track Brier scores per analyst, not raw conviction. Use calibration as the sizing input. An analyst whose 80th-percentile confidence calls hit 80% of the time is gold; an analyst whose 80% confidence hits 55% has an expensive conviction problem.
The tail-signal phenotype (A1b) is a consequence of this principle: extreme confidence is high-information only when backed by calibration. Operationalized as Recommendation 02 on slide 13.
Psychology · What makes an expert forecaster reliable 10 / 13

Tetlock: foxes beat hedgehogs. Broad, skeptical, self-updating thinkers forecast better than big-idea experts.

Philip Tetlock tracked 284 political and economic experts for 20 years — 82,361 forecasts. He found two styles (borrowed from Isaiah Berlin's essay on Tolstoy): hedgehogs who know one big thing and foxes who know many small things. Foxes won decisively. Follow-up work (the Good Judgment Project) identified "superforecasters" — they are all fox-style.
🦔 Hedgehog
"One big idea." Confident, theoretical, deductive. Organizes all evidence around a single unifying framework. More likely to appear on TV.
  • Worse forecasters — Tetlock found hedgehogs performed about the same as random chance on long-term political/economic predictions.
  • Overconfident. Forecasts they call "certain" are wrong ~20–25% of the time; they assign near-zero probability to outcomes that happen 15% of the time.
  • Fail to update. Disconfirming evidence gets reinterpreted as "noise," "timing," or "anomaly."
🦊 Fox
"Many small things." Cautious, empirical, inductive. Uses multiple frameworks, acknowledges uncertainty, updates on evidence. Less media-telegenic.
  • Better-calibrated forecasts. When a superforecaster says 70%, the base rate of occurrence is close to 70%.
  • Superforecasters (Good Judgment Project, IARPA) outperformed intelligence analysts with classified information by ~30%.
  • Habits: decompose questions, use base rates, think in ranges not points, actively seek disconfirming evidence.
Implication for RQS. The practical lesson is not "become a fox overnight" — individual disposition is stable. It is: reward fox-style forecasting in process and incentives. Structured calibration tracking (Brier scores), base-rate priors built into research templates, and explicit uncertainty intervals on analyst conviction are all fox-producing instruments.
Tetlock 2005, Expert Political Judgment: How Good Is It? How Can We Know? (Princeton) · Tetlock & Gardner 2015, Superforecasting (Crown) · goodjudgment.com (Good Judgment Inc., commercial spin-out of GJP) · Origin: Berlin 1953, from the Greek poet Archilochus: "The fox knows many things; the hedgehog knows one big thing."

Debiasing · What works vs what doesn't 12 / 13

Self-awareness alone doesn't fix bias. Process does.

Kahneman's late-career view: "I've been studying biases for 50 years and I haven't made much progress correcting mine. Organizations beat individuals." The literature backs this up — process-level interventions are where the durable evidence lives.

What doesn't (reliably) work

  • Bias-awareness training. Morewedge et al. 2015 — IARPA-funded computer games reduced several biases ~20–30% short-term. Good news. But: transfer evidence thin (one MBA field study); people with high "bias blind spot" scores benefit least (Scopelliti 2015) — that's senior PMs.
  • "Just pay attention." Haddara & Rahnev 2022 — moderate Bayesian evidence for no metacognitive-training effect under controlled conditions.
  • Post-mortems after losses. Hindsight bias makes them unreliable for updating — you learn the wrong lesson.

What does work

  • Premortem. Gary Klein: "Imagine the decision failed. Why?" Surfaces ~30% more risk factors than standard review. Does not require PFC capacity during stress.
  • Checklists with teeth. Pronovost 2006 — central-line infection checklist cut CLABSI by ~66% in Michigan ICUs. Implementation fidelity is the binding constraint (Urbach 2014 found Haynes-style surgical checklists had no benefit when rolled out without culture change).
  • Noise reduction / decision hygiene. Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein 2021 — structured decomposition, delay aggregation, independent judgments before discussion. Cuts variance in expert judgment by 30–50%.
  • Calibration tracking. Brier scores on analyst forecasts; update sizing bands using calibration, not raw confidence. The fox-producing instrument (see slide 10).
Morewedge et al. 2015, Policy Insights BBS 2(1):129 · Scopelliti et al. 2015, Mgmt Sci 61(10):2468 · Klein 2007, HBR "Performing a Project Premortem" · Pronovost et al. 2006, NEJM 355:2725 · Urbach et al. 2014, NEJM 370:1029 · Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein 2021, Noise (Little, Brown)
Recommendations · What to do with this 13 / 13

Four instruments, each grounded in mechanism and in our own data.

None of these requires any analyst or PM to be less biased. They absorb known biases as design parameters — the form of debiasing with the most durable evidence base in expert populations.
01 · From A1a (heterogeneous confidence)
Per-analyst confidence normalization
Surface percentile-within-analyst, not absolute confidence level. The biological signal (metacognitive prior precision) is only recoverable on the relative scale — cross-analyst comparison is biology-blind.
02 · From A1b (tail-signal)
Tail-weighted sizing bands & calibration tracking
Signal-detection theory predicts the tails carry more information than the middle. Size with more weight on extreme confidence, paired with Brier-score calibration tracking — the fox-producing instrument.
03 · From A1c + B1 + Hermans + Arnsten
Stress-window decision protocol
Bright-line gates on VIX / drawdown / realized-vol. Mandatory premortem + pre-committed checklist before re-allocation. Rationale: PFC capacity is lowest exactly when self-awareness would need to fire (Arnsten + Hermans) — process must precede stress, not be summoned during it.
04 · From chronobiology
Sleep & scheduling guardrails around FOMC/CPI
Avoid major re-allocations on sleep-deprived days; formalize pre-FOMC / pre-CPI guardrails. Yoo 2007 showed sleep loss amplifies amygdala response ~60% and decouples it from PFC — sleep-deprived looks neurally like acute-stressed. Parallel: opt-in wearable pilot (N≈20, pre-registered windows).
Close. The horizon collapse in our 2022 rates analyst notes is the text-output signature of a well-characterized neuroendocrine pathway. We cannot make the biology go away. We can measure it and instrument around it — four concrete tools, each with mechanism evidence and a process-level validation base. Measure → instrument → decide.
