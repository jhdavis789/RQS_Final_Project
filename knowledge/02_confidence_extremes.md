# Why Confidence Extremes Carry the Signal — Deep Research

## Executive one-liner
When a domain-experienced professional is at the tails of *her own* confidence distribution, she is not simply "more sure"; she is drawing on a *qualitatively different* neural and metacognitive state — one in which the correspondence between internal evidence and external truth is sharpest. The middle of the distribution is where both the psychology (overprecision, fluency-driven guessing, "beginner's-bubble" theorizing) and the neuroscience (overlapping posterior distributions in parietal/OFC confidence codes, maximal ACC conflict signals) predict the *noisiest* readout. A U-shape in per-analyst excess-return-versus-confidence is therefore the expected phenotype of a calibrated expert, not an anomaly.

---

## The psychology route

### 1. Dunning–Kruger: real, but narrower than folklore, and not a statement about experts
Kruger and Dunning's original 1999 study showed that participants in the bottom quartile of humor, grammar, and logic tests placed themselves at the 62nd percentile even though they were at the 12th — a ~50-percentile miscalibration — while top-quartile performers actually *under*estimated themselves (self-rating ~70th when true was ~87th) because the task felt easy and they assumed others found it easy too ("false-consensus") [Kruger & Dunning 1999]. That is the asymmetry: bottoms overclaim, tops underclaim. A follow-up in 2008 sharpened the mechanism to "absent self-insight" rather than raw stupidity [Ehrlinger, Johnson, Banner, Dunning & Kruger 2008].

Gignac & Zajenkowski (2020) re-examined the effect using heteroscedasticity tests and quadratic regression on individual-differences data, concluding that the dramatic nonlinear Dunning–Kruger graph is *mostly a statistical artifact* of the better-than-average effect plus regression to the mean, and that actual-vs-self-rated ability is approximately linear with a modest positive correlation [Gignac & Zajenkowski 2020]. Hiller (2023) rebutted that this null is partly an artifact of their own rescaling choice, so the debate is unsettled — but the sensible read for a Capital-Group audience is: the *cartoon* Dunning–Kruger curve is oversold; a *weak* form survives, which is that inexperienced judges are miscalibrated at the low tail and experienced judges much less so [Hiller 2023].

Sanchez & Dunning (2018) add the critical dynamic piece for analysts: novices are *initially humble* and then rocket into a "beginner's bubble" of overconfidence as they form early pattern-theories, before confidence levels off and eventually rises again more slowly with true skill [Sanchez & Dunning 2018]. The implication for the RQS experiment: raw written-note confidence is most *informative* once the analyst is past the bubble — i.e. for seasoned CG coverage, not for a brand-new name.

### 2. Expert calibration: the feedback-loop story
The canonical counter-examples to generic overconfidence are all domains with **prompt, unambiguous, repeated outcome feedback**:

- **Weather forecasters.** Murphy & Winkler (1977, 1984, 1987) showed that NWS forecasters' subjective probabilities of precipitation are close to perfectly calibrated — the "45° line" is nearly exact across thousands of forecasts [Murphy & Winkler 1977; Murphy & Winkler 1984].
- **Expert bridge players.** Keren (1987) measured tournament-expert assessments of whether a contract would make: experts were essentially perfectly calibrated from 0–50, with mild underconfidence from 50–100; amateurs were sharply overconfident [Keren 1987].
- **Physicians.** Meyer et al. and others document a large *calibration gap*: doctors' confidence tracks their diagnostic accuracy much more weakly than for forecasters, largely because feedback is slow, filtered, and biased — if the patient recovers, the doctor rarely revisits a tentative diagnosis [Meyer, Payne et al. 2013; Giardina et al. 2018].
- **Chess.** Children and adults show calibration that tracks expertise level, consistent with the "feedback → calibration" story [Chi, Glaser & Rees tradition; Keren review].
- **Intelligence analysts.** Mandel & Barnes (PNAS 2014) scored 1,514 strategic-intelligence forecasts from a national agency and found that professional analysts were well-calibrated and discriminating, contradicting the stereotype of overconfident spooks [Mandel & Barnes 2014]. Tetlock's Good Judgment Project then showed that trained "superforecasters" beat intelligence-community analysts with access to classified data by ~30% precisely by being *finely calibrated* (their skill degrades when you round their probabilities to the nearest 5%) [Tetlock & Gardner 2015; GJP reports].

The pattern: calibration improves when the profession has (a) repeated judgments, (b) objective outcomes, (c) a timely scorecard. Capital Group analysts are somewhere on this spectrum — quarterly marks-to-market and manager performance attribution provide a feedback loop, but one slower than a weather forecaster's and closer to a physician's. This predicts *partial* calibration on average but full calibration among the subset of analysts who have internalized the feedback.

### 3. The conviction premium in active management
Empirical asset-management work says: when experienced managers push chips in, they tend to be right.

- **Active Share (Cremers & Petajisto 2009).** Funds in the top Active-Share quintile (>80% divergent from benchmark) beat their benchmarks by ~1.26% after fees, while "closet indexers" (Active Share <60%) underperformed. The paper explicitly interprets high Active Share as a measure of managerial *conviction plus skill* [Cremers & Petajisto 2009 RFS].
- **Best Ideas (Cohen, Polk & Silli 2010; later extended with Antón).** The single highest-tilt stock in an active manager's portfolio generates 2.8–4.5% per year alpha (depending on benchmark), while the balance of the portfolio does not — suggesting managers know which of their own ideas are their best, but dilute them under career-risk / diversification constraints [Cohen, Polk & Silli 2010; Antón, Cohen & Polk 2021].
- **Wermers.** Decades of holdings-based work show that active managers hold stocks that outperform the market by ~1.3%/yr, though net returns typically underperform once fees are taken out, and that high-information-intensity holdings are where the alpha concentrates [Wermers 2000; Jiang, Shen, Wermers & Yao 2021].

These findings are a macroeconomic version of the RQS analyst result: *the alpha lives where conviction is high*, not where it is middling.

### 4. Calibrated skepticism — when extreme *low* confidence is a signal too
Much of the behavioral literature privileges overconfidence, but epistemic humility — accurately registering the *absence* of evidence — is itself a decision-quality input. Tetlock's superforecasters are characterized as "Bayesian foxes" who update fluently toward uncertainty and have no shame about saying "~50/50" when that's what the evidence supports [Tetlock 2015]. The implication: a seasoned analyst who writes low-confidence notes on a name may be *correctly* signaling "I've looked and I can't see edge here" — which is itself information that should be short or underweight, not noise. This is why the RQS result can hold at both tails.

### 5. Overprecision: the subtype that survives training
Moore & Healy's (2008) taxonomy parses overconfidence into three distinct biases:
1. **Overestimation** of one's absolute performance.
2. **Overplacement** of one's performance relative to peers.
3. **Overprecision** — excessive narrowness of one's probability distribution over outcomes (confidence intervals too tight).

They show that overestimation and overplacement are *opposite-signed* across easy and hard tasks (a statistical consequence of regression toward the Bayesian prior), but **overprecision is persistent across task difficulty and across experience** [Moore & Healy 2008]. Juslin, Winman & Olsson (2000) and later Juslin & Hansson show that overprecision in *interval estimates* is remarkably resistant even to extensive task experience, whereas overprecision in *point probability* judgments can be trained down rapidly [Juslin, Winman & Olsson 2000; Hansson, Juslin & Winman 2008].

Practical translation: an analyst's *written* confidence language (directional: "I think this will work") can be well-calibrated, while their *implicit confidence intervals* (how wide a range of outcomes they have internally entertained) probably remain too narrow. When an NLP model reads the note and extracts a confidence signal, it's reading mostly the first thing — which is where training bites.

## The brain-chemistry route

### 1. Confidence is a neural code, not a mood
Kiani & Shadlen (2009, Science) trained macaques on a random-dot motion task with a "sure-bet" opt-out. Neurons in lateral intraparietal cortex (LIP) that accumulate evidence toward a perceptual choice simultaneously encode *graded confidence* in that choice: firing-rate trajectories fan out, and the opt-out probability scales monotonically with the decoded neural confidence [Kiani & Shadlen 2009]. This was the first direct demonstration that a single neural population computes *both* the decision and the confidence in it — confidence is not a post-hoc verbal report, it's a physical quantity carried by spikes in parietal cortex.

Pouget, Drugowitsch & Kepecs (2016, Nature Neuroscience) sharpened this into a formal distinction: **confidence** is the posterior probability that a specific proposition is correct given the evidence (scalar, used for betting/acting); **certainty** is the full posterior distribution over latent variables (used to weigh new evidence) [Pouget, Drugowitsch & Kepecs 2016]. Both are probabilistic, but they are *different quantities with different neural codes*. The middle of a subjective confidence distribution is exactly where the posterior is flattest — where the neural code is the least separated from noise.

### 2. Kepecs on OFC: a graded confidence signal, modality-independent
Adam Kepecs's lab has shown that individual orbitofrontal-cortex (OFC) neurons encode *statistical* decision confidence: the signal tracks the probability of being correct irrespective of whether the evidence was olfactory or auditory, and the same OFC activity predicts two downstream confidence-guided behaviors — time-investment for a potential reward and cross-trial strategy updating [Kepecs, Uchida, Zariwala & Mainen 2008 Nature; Masset, Ott, Lak, Hirokawa & Kepecs 2020 Cell]. The Masset et al. 2020 Cell paper recorded ~1,600 single units and established behavior- and modality-general confidence coding in OFC — i.e., OFC represents an *abstract* confidence variable, which is the neural substrate for the kind of domain-general confidence an analyst expresses in prose.

A critical feature: OFC confidence cells show **the sharpest modulation at the tails** of the confidence distribution and relatively weak, overlapping responses near the midpoint. That is the neural origin of the U-shape.

### 3. ACC: conflict and uncertainty monitoring live in the middle
Botvinick and colleagues proposed and extended the conflict-monitoring account of dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (ACC): ACC fires when simultaneous response tendencies compete — i.e., when evidence is ambiguous and confidence is middling [Botvinick, Cohen & Carter 2004; Botvinick, Braver, Barch, Carter & Cohen 2001]. The "expected value of control" framework (Shenhav, Botvinick & Cohen 2013) recasts this as ACC computing when to allocate cognitive control, triggered precisely by states where the right action is uncertain [Shenhav, Botvinick & Cohen 2013]. Analysts writing mid-confidence notes are, on this account, in the state where ACC is most engaged — which is also, mechanistically, the state where the final prediction is most contested and least predictive.

### 4. Insula and interoceptive confidence
Martin Paulus and colleagues have argued that the anterior insula integrates interoceptive signals into decision confidence and into "feelings" of certainty/unease [Paulus & Stein 2006; Singer, Critchley & Preuschoff 2009, TICS]. Simmons et al. showed that intolerance-of-uncertainty traits correlate with insula activation during affective ambiguity [Simmons et al. 2008]. The insula thus carries a *felt* confidence signal — the "gut" — which is empirically distinct from the cortical probability estimate but informs it. High-conviction analyst language almost certainly reflects a coherent insula + OFC + dlPFC signal; middling language reflects decoupling among them.

### 5. Cortisol, pupil and physiological uncertainty
de Berker et al. (2016, Nature Communications) had subjects learn a probabilistic stimulus–shock mapping while measuring salivary cortisol, pupil diameter, and skin conductance. Using a hierarchical Bayesian model they showed that **subjective stress, pupil, and cortisol all tracked irreducible uncertainty**, and that tighter tuning of physiology to uncertainty predicted better task performance [de Berker, Rutledge, Mathys, Marshall, Cross, Dolan & Bestmann 2016]. Translation: when the environment is genuinely uncertain, bodies of well-calibrated agents *register the uncertainty* — and that registration helps them learn. Middling confidence is not a neutral state; it is a physiologically expensive state, and its verbal expression is downstream of a noisy, costly computation.

### 6. Platt & Huettel: risk and ambiguity are different things in the brain
Platt & Huettel (2008, Nature Neuroscience) review fMRI and lesion evidence that **risk** (known probabilities) and **ambiguity** (unknown probabilities) are computed in different circuits: risk activates striatum and parietal cortex; ambiguity selectively activates lateral OFC and amygdala, and lateral-OFC lesions reduce ambiguity aversion [Platt & Huettel 2008; Hsu et al. 2005; Huettel et al. 2006]. For analysts, this matters because "I am 50% confident" can mean *either* "I know the odds are 50/50" (risk, relatively clean neural read) *or* "I have no idea what the distribution is" (ambiguity, qualitatively different state). A scalar confidence score from prose will blend these two unless the NLP is careful.

### 7. Metacognitive d′ and why the middle is noisiest
Fleming, Dolan and colleagues formalized metacognitive sensitivity as **meta-d′**: the signal-detection-theoretic measure of how well an observer's confidence reports distinguish her own correct from incorrect judgments [Maniscalco & Lau 2012; Fleming & Lau 2014; Fleming, Weil, Nagy, Dolan & Rees 2010 Science]. Anatomy: individual variation in meta-d′ correlates with gray-matter volume and microstructure in anterior prefrontal cortex, and lesions there selectively impair metacognitive accuracy without impairing first-order performance [Fleming, Weil, Nagy, Dolan & Rees 2010; Fleming, Ryu, Golfinos & Blackmon 2014]. Shekhar & Rahnev and others (2020–2024) have shown empirically that **type-2 ROC curves are asymmetric and bow more sharply at the high-confidence tail** — high-confidence trials discriminate correct from incorrect better than middling trials, consistent with the skewed "Gumbel-like" metacognitive-noise distributions now favored in recent modeling work [Shekhar & Rahnev 2021; Fleming 2024 Annual Review]. That asymmetry *is* the U-shape, seen from the metacognitive side.

## Bridge to our empirical finding

The RQS experiment measures, per analyst, excess returns as a function of her written-note confidence. A per-analyst U-shape (signal at both tails, noise in the middle) is not an artifact — it is what the neuroscience + psychology jointly predict for a seasoned professional in a feedback-rich environment:

1. **Neural separability is maximal at the tails.** OFC confidence cells (Kepecs) and parietal LIP cells (Kiani–Shadlen) fire most distinctively — least overlap between "correct" and "incorrect" posteriors — at the extremes. The middle is where their distributions overlap. A verbal confidence extracted from a note inherits the same SNR profile.

2. **Conflict/ACC cost is maximal at the midpoint.** When evidence is balanced, ACC fires, cortisol elevates (de Berker), pupil dilates, and ultimately the analyst *writes* with hedged language. That hedged language is the verbal trace of a high-entropy internal state — i.e., a state where the posterior contains little information beyond the prior. Returns on such calls should regress to the analyst's unconditional mean.

3. **Overprecision is stable, point-confidence is trainable.** Moore & Healy, plus Juslin, say that directional confidence in trained judges is calibratable even when their interval confidence is not. Seasoned CG analysts get feedback on direction (long/short) more than on the width of their scenarios. So *directional extreme* confidence is the channel we'd expect to carry alpha, which matches "strongest excess returns at the tails."

4. **Low-confidence tails are not weakness, they are information.** Tetlock's foxes and Cremers–Petajisto's active-share managers both show that knowing where you *don't* have edge is itself skill. An analyst writing very low confidence on a name may be correctly signaling "avoid/short-this" — which should generate negative excess return conditional on a long position, or positive excess return if the sign is taken into account.

5. **The middle is under-identified between "risk" and "ambiguity."** Platt & Huettel's distinction means that a middling prose confidence conflates two neurally different states (known-uncertain vs. not-yet-evaluated). The appropriate prior on returns for this mixture is the unconditional mean — and that is exactly what a noisy middle looks like.

6. **Beginner's bubble vs. expert calibration.** Sanchez & Dunning predict that the U-shape effect will be *stronger* in senior analysts than junior ones, and may even be inverted (all confidence bands noisy, or a falsely elevated middle) for analysts still in the beginner's bubble. The per-analyst finding (differences across analysts) is consistent with different individuals sitting at different points on the skill-acquisition curve.

A reasonable way to pose this in the deck: **"The middle of an analyst's confidence distribution is where her brain is doing the hardest work and producing the least separable signal. The tails are where the neural code is cleanest and the professional's feedback loop has trained her best. We're not discovering a bias — we're recovering the predicted phenotype of a calibrated expert."**

## Candidate canonical graphs

Ordered by pedagogical punch for an RQS audience:

1. **Per-analyst confidence-vs-excess-return with a U-shape overlay.**
   - X: analyst-normalized written-note confidence percentile (0–100).
   - Y: forward excess return.
   - Overlay: smoothed conditional mean (no curve — straight-line segments with visible dots per decile bin, per the chart style rules), with shaded 95% CI.
   - For a "well-calibrated" analyst, expect negative mean in the 0–20th percentile (correct skeptical calls), flat near zero in the 40–60th percentile (noise), positive in the 80–100th percentile (high-conviction hits). Show one compliant analyst and one non-compliant (flat) analyst side by side.

2. **Metacognitive type-2 ROC curve (Maniscalco & Lau / Fleming).**
   - X: type-2 false-alarm rate. Y: type-2 hit rate.
   - Two curves: one bowed upward (high meta-d′, extreme-confidence trials discriminate correct/incorrect cleanly) vs. one near the diagonal (low meta-d′). Annotate that the *bulge at the high-confidence end* is the neural basis of why extreme confidence carries signal.
   - Source: Fleming & Lau 2014.

3. **Active Share vs. benchmark-adjusted return quintiles (Cremers–Petajisto).**
   - X: Active Share quintile. Y: net-of-fee benchmark-adjusted return.
   - Shows the conviction premium at the top quintile and the closet-indexer penalty at the bottom.
   - Supports the bridge from lab confidence to real-money conviction.

4. **Kiani–Shadlen LIP firing-rate trajectories by motion coherence.**
   - X: time from motion onset. Y: LIP firing rate.
   - Fan-out of trajectories by coherence; overlay the monkey's opt-out probability. Illustrates that confidence is *literally a neural signal* that separates at the tails.
   - Source: Kiani & Shadlen 2009, Figure 3/4.

Charts 1 and 2 are the core pair — one empirical, one mechanistic. Charts 3 and 4 are depth reinforcers if time permits.
