# Stress, Cortisol, and Time Horizon Compression — Deep Research

## Executive one-liner
When portfolios sell off, the decision-maker's brain does not just "feel bad" — it undergoes a measurable neuroendocrine reconfiguration (HPA-axis cortisol surge, catecholamine flooding of the prefrontal cortex, amygdala/striatum disinhibition) whose well-documented behavioral consequences are *exactly* what we observe in the 2022 analyst transcripts: a sharp compression of evaluation horizon, elevated loss aversion at that shorter horizon, and a shift from deliberative (goal-directed, long-horizon, PFC-dependent) toward reactive (habitual, short-horizon, striatal) decision modes. The written horizon compression is a textual signature of the same physiological event that made 2022 traders and portfolio managers sell the low.

## The psychology route

### 1. Prospect theory and the loss-aversion coefficient (~2x)
Kahneman and Tversky (1979) established that human choice under risk is not over final wealth states but over *gains and losses* relative to a reference point, and that the value function is kinked: steeper for losses than for gains. In Tversky and Kahneman's (1992) follow-up, cumulative prospect theory, the median loss-aversion coefficient λ was estimated at **2.25** — losses are felt roughly 2.25x as intensely as equivalent gains. This parameter is the quantitative bedrock of every subsequent behavioral-finance result about how investors respond to drawdowns. A recent meta-analysis across dozens of risky-choice studies continues to recover λ in the 1.8–2.1 range, so the "~2x" rule is robust, not an artifact of a single paper.

### 2. Myopic loss aversion — shorter horizons punish risky assets more
Benartzi and Thaler (1995, *QJE* 110(1):73–92) solved the equity premium puzzle by combining prospect-theory loss aversion with the observation that investors evaluate portfolios *frequently*. They showed that the historical equity premium is consistent with prospect-theory parameters if investors mentally close their books about **once per year**. The mechanism is combinatorial: with a one-year horizon, annual equity returns are negative roughly one year in three and those losses are felt 2.25x, generating a large required risk premium; with a 30-year horizon, the probability of a negative cumulative return is near zero and the loss-aversion penalty essentially disappears. The Benartzi–Thaler finding inverts directly into our setting: **any force that shortens the evaluation horizon mechanically amplifies effective loss aversion and depresses apparent tolerance for duration / credit / equity risk**, even if true preferences have not changed at all. This is why "horizon compression" is not a harmless rhetorical shift — it is economically equivalent to a spike in risk aversion.

### 3. Drawdown-induced horizon compression in investors
- **Disposition effect (Shefrin & Statman 1985, *JF* 40(3):777–790; Odean 1998, *JF* 53(5):1775–1798):** investors sell winners too quickly and hold losers too long. Odean's account-level data on 10,000 brokerage accounts found investors were **1.5–2x more likely** to realize gains than equivalent losses, controlling for taxes and rebalancing. In prospect-theory language, realized losers are kept at their original reference point to preserve the chance of "breakeven," while winners' reference points reset and selling them locks in a felt-good gain.
- **Underreaction and post-loss inertia (Frazzini 2006, *JF* 61(4):2017–2046):** the disposition effect generates *underreaction to news*, especially bad news on stocks trading at a large capital loss. Losses distort both *what* investors do and *when* they process information — they freeze, and horizon collapses to "when does this stop hurting."
- **Coates & Herbert (2008, *PNAS*) and Cueva et al. (2015, *Sci Rep*):** field and lab studies of traders document cortisol rising with market volatility and, crucially, sustained cortisol elevation causing a **44% drop in the "risk premium" participants demand** — but in the direction of *more* risk aversion, i.e. horizon compression translated into demanded compensation. This is empirical confirmation that drawdowns physiologically reconfigure the same decision variables prospect theory describes.

### 4. Easterbrook's cue-utilization hypothesis — arousal narrows attention
Easterbrook (1959, *Psychol. Review*) proposed that increasing emotional arousal **progressively narrows the range of cues an organism utilizes**. At low arousal attention is diffuse; at moderate arousal irrelevant peripheral cues are filtered out (performance-enhancing); at high arousal even task-relevant but non-central cues are excluded (performance-impairing). Modern reviews extend Easterbrook specifically to threat: threat — not arousal per se — narrows the attentional spotlight. Mapped onto investing, a loss is a threat cue, so in drawdowns the analyst's spotlight contracts: long-horizon fundamentals (terminal value, strategic positioning, 10-year cash flows) are *peripheral*; the P&L tape is *central*. The attentional field physically shrinks around the loss.

### 5. Delay discounting under stress — Kimura 2013 and the Lempert line
- Kimura et al. (2013, *Psychoneuroendocrinology* 38(10):2300–2308) administered the Trier Social Stress Test and measured delay discounting before and after. Discounting rates **rose only in cortisol responders**, pinpointing the HPA axis (not generic "stress") as the causal driver. The pathway the authors propose — HPA → dopaminergic systems → valuation — is the exact mechanism we invoke.
- Lempert et al. (2012, *Frontiers in Psychology* 3:251) showed individual differences in trait perceived stress moderate the effect: anticipatory acute stress raised delay discounting rate as a function of trait stress. Again, increased cortisol moved subjects toward **smaller, sooner rewards**.
- A 2024 meta-analysis (Fields et al.) and the Haushofer & Fehr (2014) *Science* review both note the effect is real but time-dependent: present-bias increases within minutes of cortisol elevation, with a genomic second wave at 60–90 minutes. For our purposes the punchline is robust: cortisol elevation shifts subjects toward nearer rewards, i.e. a compressed time horizon in valuation.
- Porcelli & Delgado (2009, *Psych. Sci.* 20:278–283) showed the reflection effect — risk-aversion in gains, risk-seeking in losses — *amplifies* under acute stress. Drawdown-stressed analysts don't just shorten their horizon; they become more gambling-prone about near-term losses (hold and hope) while more conservative about near-term gains (sell the rally). This is the disposition effect in its acute, stress-potentiated form.

### 6. Was 2022 unusual? Yes, at an historic scale
- The Bloomberg US Treasury Index returned **–12.5% in 2022**, its worst calendar year since inception in 1973.
- Intermediate Treasuries lost **–10.6%**, the worst on record for data going back to 1926.
- Long-duration zero-coupon Treasuries lost **–39.2%** in 2022 — a record low in data stretching to 1754.
- The Bloomberg Global Aggregate fell 11% from its January-2021 peak, the largest drawdown in the index's modern history, exceeding 2008.
- Pace matters: the Fed tightened **425 bps in 9 months** starting March 2022 (50, 75, 75, 75, 75, 50, 50 bp cadence), the fastest pace since 1980. 1994 — the prior archetype "bond massacre" — was 300 bp over roughly 12 months, and did not see the same absolute price damage because starting yields were far higher (yield cushion). 2022 was worse than 1994, 2013 (taper tantrum, ~100 bp backup in 10y yield), or 2020 (COVID spasm, largely a March blowout then retrace). For a rates analyst specifically, 2022 was a once-in-a-career, and plausibly once-in-many-careers, cortisol-eliciting event.

## The brain-chemistry route

### 1. HPA-axis activation under loss — the cascade and its timescales
A salient loss or threat activates the **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis**: the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus releases CRH → anterior pituitary releases ACTH → adrenal cortex releases **cortisol**. In parallel, the sympathoadrenal pathway fires within seconds, flooding the brain with **norepinephrine and dopamine** from brainstem catecholaminergic nuclei (locus coeruleus, VTA). Timescales: catecholamine surge in seconds; cortisol peaks at roughly 20–30 minutes post-stressor; non-genomic cortisol effects within 15–30 minutes; slower genomic effects at 60–90+ minutes (Joëls & Baram review; Haushofer & Fehr 2014). This is why the behavioral shift in a trading desk under a selloff is both immediate and sustained — two systems, two timescales, compounding.

### 2. Catecholamines and the PFC — Arnsten's inverted-U
Arnsten's 2009 *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* paper "Stress signalling pathways that impair prefrontal cortex structure and function" is the canonical source. The key findings:
- The prefrontal cortex is uniquely vulnerable: its pyramidal neurons rely on precisely tuned **D1 dopamine and α2A-noradrenergic receptor** signaling to maintain persistent "delay-period" activity — the neural substrate of working memory and long-horizon planning.
- Catecholamine action on PFC follows an **inverted-U**: moderate levels strengthen signal (α2A) and suppress noise (D1); very high levels (acute uncontrollable stress) engage **α1-adrenergic and D1 cAMP-HCN signaling** that *open HCN channels and shunt away* the very currents that keep PFC cells firing. The PFC effectively goes offline.
- Simultaneously, top-down PFC inhibition of the **amygdala and dorsal striatum** is lost. The amygdala gains free rein (fear, loss sensitivity); the striatum gains free rein (habit, stimulus–response).
- This is *mild acute* stress. Chronic stress (section 4) actually remodels the tissue.

### 3. Cortisol → delay discounting at the mechanism level
Stress research converges on cortisol as the *causal* neuroendocrine mediator of the present-bias shift:
- Kimura et al. (2013) isolated cortisol responders as the sub-population whose delay discounting rose after the TSST.
- Haushofer & Fehr (2014, *Science* 344:862–867) review evidence that cortisol elevation causes **short-sighted, risk-averse decision-making**, possibly by limiting attention (Easterbrook) and **favoring habitual over goal-directed action** (Schwabe & Wolf, section 6).
- The proposed circuit: cortisol modulates the VTA–nucleus accumbens dopaminergic valuation pathway, upweighting the value of **immediate reward** and downweighting delayed reward. Concretely, this is what an fMRI study of intertemporal choice picks up as steeper hyperbolic discount slope.
- A sustained-cortisol regime (Cueva et al., 2015; Kandasamy et al., 2014, *PNAS* 111:3608–3613) *increases* the risk premium demanded — i.e., at the aggregate level, bidding down risky assets at exactly the wrong time.

### 4. Acute vs. chronic — different neural signatures
- **Acute stress** (minutes to hours): catecholamine flood + cortisol surge → functional disconnection of PFC, amygdala/striatum release. Reversible.
- **Chronic stress** (days to weeks): McEwen and colleagues (reviewed in *Neuron*, 2013, 79:16–29) show *structural* change: in rodent models, chronic restraint stress produces ~**20% reduction in apical dendritic length** and ~**16% reduction in spine density** in medial PFC pyramidal neurons, with roughly **one-third of axospinous synapses lost**. The amygdala, in contrast, *grows* dendrites under the same stress. The brain literally rewires toward threat detection and away from executive planning.
- Sousa and Almeida (2012, *Trends in Neurosciences* 35:742–751) frame this as "disconnection and reconnection": chronic stress disintegrates the PFC → striatum/amygdala regulatory network and strengthens subcortical loops. Crucially, in young animals the atrophy is reversible after stress ends; in middle-aged it is blunted; in aged animals recovery is absent.
- Implication for our analysts: 2022 was not a one-day event. It was a *sustained* 9-month bond selloff. By Q3–Q4 we are past acute and into subchronic territory, where structural PFC effects begin to accumulate. The horizon compression we see in late-2022 text is plausibly not just a state marker but a trait shift over the period.

### 5. Amygdala, framing, and anticipatory anxiety
De Martino, Kumaran, Seymour & Dolan (2006, *Science* 313:684–687) scanned subjects making framed choices (identical gambles labeled as "keep X" vs "lose X"). The framing effect — choosing the sure thing in "keep" framings, gambling in "lose" framings — was specifically associated with **amygdala activity**, while subjects less susceptible to framing showed greater orbital/medial PFC activity. This is the neuroimaging face of the reflection effect (Porcelli & Delgado): when top-down PFC control is weak and amygdala is hot, decisions become frame-captured. In a drawdown, every analyst memo is implicitly framed in the loss domain, and the amygdala is hot.

### 6. Goal-directed → habitual shift (Schwabe & Wolf)
Schwabe and Wolf (2009, *J. Neurosci.* 29(22):7191–7198, "Stress Prompts Habit Behavior in Humans") ran the gold-standard outcome-devaluation paradigm: train subjects on an instrumental action, then devalue its outcome. Goal-directed controllers stop doing the action; habit controllers keep doing it. **Stressed subjects kept acting regardless of outcome value** — their behavior had moved from the PFC-dependent goal-directed system to the dorsolateral striatum habit system. A 2011 follow-up (Schwabe et al., *J. Neurosci.* 31:17317) showed that a **β-adrenergic antagonist (propranolol) blocks the stress-induced shift** — i.e., the mechanism is catecholamine-mediated, exactly the pathway Arnsten identified. Habits are by definition short-horizon, stimulus-bound, and insensitive to distal consequences. This is the neural vocabulary for "analyst who last quarter wrote about 2030 terminal value is now writing about next month's meeting."

## The feedback loop

The 2022 story is not a static shock. It is a *self-amplifying loop*:

1. **Drawdown** (bonds selling off) → salient loss signal.
2. **HPA activation** → cortisol spike (20–30 min) and catecholamine surge (seconds).
3. **PFC offline** → working memory and top-down control of amygdala/striatum impaired (Arnsten 2009).
4. **Attentional narrowing** (Easterbrook 1959) → peripheral/long-horizon cues dropped.
5. **Delay discounting rises** (Kimura 2013; Haushofer & Fehr 2014) → valuation function tilts toward the near.
6. **Evaluation horizon collapses** from years to months to days.
7. **At the shorter horizon, loss aversion is mechanically amplified** (Benartzi & Thaler 1995) — a 1-month loss window shows losses three out of ten windows; a 10-year window shows zero.
8. **Shift from goal-directed to habitual control** (Schwabe & Wolf 2009) → reactive trading, sell the low, buy the bounce.
9. **These behaviors worsen realized P&L** → more drawdown → back to step 1.
10. **Sustained cortisol** over weeks begins structural PFC remodeling (McEwen; Sousa & Almeida 2012) — the *trait* level of horizon shortens, not just the state.

Additionally, the loop is social: traders on the same desk, analysts on the same coverage team, share the tape, share the headlines, and HPA-activate in synchrony. Cueva et al. (2015) and Coates & Herbert (2008) document aggregate cortisol spikes across trading floors, meaning *market-wide* horizon compression is a real phenomenon that can feed back into prices — risk premia get bid up exactly when fundamentals say they should be bid down.

This loop is what the analyst transcripts are a window onto. It is not a story about bad analysts or a bad culture; it is a story about a specific, well-characterized physiological response running on general-purpose human hardware, in an environment that maximally triggers it.

## Bridge to our empirical finding

The RQS analyst experiment recorded, in 2022, a systematic shortening of written time-horizon framing across multiple rates analysts. The experiment reports text, but what the text measures is the *output* of the decision-making apparatus. Given the literature above, this output change is *exactly* the expected phenotype of the HPA-cortisol-catecholamine cascade under a record bond drawdown:

- **Horizon compression** → predicted by cortisol-induced rise in delay discounting (Kimura 2013), by Easterbrook attentional narrowing, and by Schwabe–Wolf goal-directed-to-habitual shift.
- **Cross-analyst consistency** → predicted because the mechanism is a species-general neuroendocrine response, not a quirk of any individual. Analogous to pupil dilation: everyone's does it under threat.
- **Severity-matched** → 2022 was the worst rates drawdown in the modern dataset, so the dose is the largest in the career of every analyst. Dose–response should yield the largest horizon compression ever recorded, which is consistent with what the experiment found.
- **Language-level channel** → written horizon framing is known to track attentional focus (Easterbrook). If the attentional spotlight contracts, the writing naturally reports on what's in it.

The right framing for the presentation is: **the written horizon shrinkage is a behavioral biomarker of a neuroendocrine event**. Just as resting cortisol in saliva or pupil diameter or heart-rate variability would have shown the same stress signature in 2022 traders, the analysts' text shows it in prose form. The experiment has, in effect, measured portfolio cortisol via language — a cheap, non-invasive, retrospective proxy for an acute HPA episode.

The practical implication — which is where the presentation can land — is that if horizon compression under drawdown is a physiological reflex rather than a considered judgment, then the *institutional* remedy is precommitment-style devices that decouple decision horizon from affective state: pre-registered investment theses with explicit horizon declarations, drawdown circuit breakers that defer decisions past the cortisol peak, peer review by non-affected analysts, and systematic rebalancing that removes the discretion entirely when cortisol would otherwise be ruling.
