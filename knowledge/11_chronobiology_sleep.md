# Chronobiology, Sleep, and Trader Performance — Deep Research

## Executive one-liner
Sleep is the single most under-priced input to the investment process: one night of total sleep deprivation amplifies amygdala reactivity to negative stimuli by ~60% and severs its functional connectivity to the medial prefrontal cortex ([Yoo, Gujar, Hu, Jolesz, Walker 2007, *Current Biology*](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822%2807%2901783-6)) — i.e. a single short night reproduces, in a rested brain, the exact neural phenotype we have been attributing to acute market stress, and the intervention lever (go to bed earlier, don't schedule major reallocations after redeyes) is free, behavioral, and immediately implementable at the desk and policy level.

## Sleep deprivation and PFC — Yoo et al. and the amygdala–PFC uncoupling

**The central study.** Yoo, Gujar, Hu, Jolesz, and Walker (2007, *Current Biology* 17:R877–R878) kept 26 healthy young adults awake for ~35 hours and, with an age- and sex-matched rested control arm, scanned them during an emotional picture-viewing task (100 images ranging from neutral to increasingly aversive). Two findings are the load-bearing pillars of the "sleep and behavioral bias" story.

1. **Amygdala reactivity to aversive stimuli was ~60% greater in the sleep-deprived group** than in the rested controls (the paper reports a 60% amplification in the amygdala BOLD response to the most aversive stimuli). The dynamic range of the amygdala — how it titrates its response to escalating threat — also widened: sleep-deprived subjects recruited ~three times as much amygdala tissue as rested controls.
2. **The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) decoupled from the amygdala.** Functional connectivity analyses (psychophysiological interaction) showed that, in rested subjects, mPFC and amygdala co-varied negatively during aversive processing — the signature of PFC top-down regulation of limbic reactivity. In sleep-deprived subjects this relationship *inverted:* amygdala connectivity with mPFC was significantly reduced, while amygdala connectivity with the autonomic-output locus coeruleus and brainstem targets strengthened.

The causal interpretation: sleep loss removes the cortical brake. The same limbic machinery that an acute cortisol/catecholamine surge knocks offline (see file 04) is knocked offline by a single sleeplessness exposure, via different biochemistry but the same circuit-level outcome. For a presentation that has already walked the audience through Arnsten's stress → PFC-offline story, **sleep deprivation is the same pathology arriving through the front door instead of the back.**

**Replications and extensions.** Motomura et al. (2013, *PLoS ONE* 8:e56578) replicated the amygdala hyper-reactivity finding in a 5-day partial sleep restriction protocol — i.e. this is not just a "pulled an all-nighter" artefact. Goldstein & Walker (2014, *Annu. Rev. Clin. Psychol.* 10:679–708) give the review of the amygdala-hyperreactivity-without-PFC-control mechanism across TSD (total sleep deprivation), PSR (partial sleep restriction), and clinical insomnia populations. The phenomenon is robust.

**Harrison & Horne 2000 — PFC-specific impairment.** Harrison and Horne ([2000, *J Exp Psychol Appl* 6:236–249](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11014057/)) argued, before the fMRI era caught up, that sleep deprivation is not a generic cognitive impairment — it is *specifically* a prefrontal impairment. Simple, well-practiced tasks (reaction time, arithmetic) degrade slowly or not at all; tasks that require novel reasoning, divergent thinking, temporal memory, behavioral flexibility, appreciation of updated information, and realistic risk assessment degrade fast. Their review covers Wisconsin Card Sorting performance, Hayling Sentence Completion, word fluency, Tower of London — all PFC-dependent, all impaired after one night awake even when subjects self-rate as "fine." This is the seminal articulation of the principle that **sleep deprivation selectively impairs exactly the functions a PM needs most.**

**McKenna, Dickinson, Orff, Drummond 2007 — Iowa Gambling Task.** McKenna et al. ([2007, *J Sleep Res* 16:245–252](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17716272/)) gave sleep-deprived subjects a modified Iowa Gambling Task — the standard clinical task for probing vmPFC-mediated value integration. Sleep-deprived subjects made *more* risky choices than rested controls for *gains* (chasing high-variance wins) and *fewer* risky choices for *losses* (avoiding high-variance hits). The asymmetry is exactly the reflection-effect pattern Tversky-Kahneman prospect theory predicts — and it **amplifies under sleep deprivation in the same direction as it amplifies under acute stress** (Porcelli & Delgado 2009, cited in file 03). Two independent perturbations, same phenotype, pointing at the same underlying circuit.

**Killgore and risk-taking.** William Killgore's group has published a series of sleep-deprivation and decision-making studies. Killgore et al. ([2006, *Sleep* 29:1091–1095](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16944647/)) showed that 49.5 hours awake increased risk-taking on the Balloon Analog Risk Task (BART) with no corresponding increase in perceived risk — subjects felt no more risk-tolerant, yet behaved more risk-tolerant. Killgore, Balkin, & Wesensten ([2006, *J Sleep Res* 15:7–13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16489997/)) documented degraded moral judgment after 53 hours without sleep — specifically, the capacity to integrate emotional and cognitive inputs to moral dilemmas, which is a classic vmPFC function. Killgore ([2010, *Prog Brain Res* 185:105–129](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21075236/)) is the review paper tying these threads together: sleep deprivation selectively impairs the integration of emotion, reward, and higher-order judgment — the exact stack that portfolio decisions require.

**Walker's *Why We Sleep* — useful framing, primary sources preferred.** Matthew Walker's book ([2017, Scribner](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Why-We-Sleep/Matthew-Walker/9781501144325)) is the mainstream framing but has been challenged on sourcing ([Alexey Guzey's critique, 2019](https://guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/)) — for the RQS audience, cite the primary literature (Yoo, Harrison & Horne, McKenna, Killgore, Van Dongen) and use Walker sparingly for synthesis.

## Time-of-day effects on cognition and risk-taking

**The circadian backbone.** The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus, entrained by retinal light input, is the master clock. Its ~24-hour output oscillation shapes core body temperature, cortisol, melatonin, autonomic tone, and — downstream of all of those — cognitive performance. The Schmidt, Collette, Cajochen, Peigneux review ([2007, *Cognitive Neuropsychology* 24:755–789](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18066734/)) remains the most-cited synthesis: performance on sustained attention and working memory tracks a composite of homeostatic sleep pressure (rising across waking hours) and circadian alertness (~24h oscillation), with a **"forbidden zone" of low cognitive output around 02:00–05:00 local time** and a **"wake-maintenance zone" of peak alertness around 20:00–22:00** for most chronotypes — slightly earlier for morning types, later for evening types.

**The cortisol awakening response (CAR) and the diurnal cortisol curve.** This is the critical cross-reference to the endocrinology agent. Cortisol rises **50–160% in the 30–45 minutes after waking** ([Pruessner, Wolf, Hellhammer et al. 1997, *Life Sciences* 61:2539–2549](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9416776/); [Clow, Thorn, Evans, Hucklebridge 2004, *Stress* 7:29–37](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15204030/); [Fries, Dettenborn, Kirschbaum 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18854200/)). Baseline cortisol then declines slowly across the day to a trough near midnight. Superimposed on this macro curve is ultradian pulsatility (~1 pulse/hour).

For a PM sitting at the desk at 09:30 ET, this is not an abstraction: **your endogenous cortisol is at the single highest point of its 24-hour range** exactly when the US cash equity open happens. If you then add a headline-driven acute HPA activation (sell-off, macro surprise, earnings shock), you are starting from a much higher floor than if the same shock hit at 15:00. The time-of-day of a decision is itself a cortisol dose.

**Peak cognition vs. peak risk-taking — not the same clock.** Kahneman (in his *Thinking Fast and Slow* synthesis) popularized the finding that deliberate "System 2" reasoning performance peaks mid-morning for morning types and degrades across the day as ego-depletion and homeostatic sleep pressure build. But risk-taking is *not* the inverse of cognition — it is separately modulated.

Bodenhausen ([1990, *Psychol Sci* 1:319–322](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1990.tb00226.x)) showed that stereotype-based judgment — a proxy for heuristic, low-effort cognition — spikes at subjects' circadian non-peak times. In morning types, heuristic judgment is worst in the evening. In evening types, heuristic judgment is worst in the morning. The implication: **time-of-day alignment with chronotype matters more than absolute clock time.**

May, Hasher, Stoltzfus ([1993, *Psychol Sci* 4:326–330](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7737651/)) documented the same for inhibitory control — morning types show more intrusive off-task thoughts in the evening; evening types show more in the morning. If your analyst is a confirmed night owl and you mandate 07:00 risk-committee meetings, you are systematically catching them in their inhibitory-control trough.

**Risk-taking is elevated in the evening for most people.** Byrnes, Miller, Schafer meta-analysis ([1999, *Psychol Bull* 125:367–383](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10320707/)) aggregates gender and age effects but also touches time-of-day. More pointedly, Bedrosian & Nelson ([2013, *Nat Neurosci* 16:1265–1266](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn.3508)) and subsequent work have shown that **evening hours, under dim light, are associated with higher risk-taking on experimental tasks** — an effect attributed to melatonin-driven disinhibition of dopaminergic reward circuits. For a trading desk, the fact that Asia-session coverage and overnight rebalancing happen in this window is worth noting.

## Sleep deprivation and the Iowa Gambling Task / risky choice

**The Duke study — Venkatraman et al. 2007.** Venkatraman, Chuah, Huettel, Chee ([2007, *Sleep* 30:603–609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17552375/)) ran an economic decision task under fMRI on 26 adults after a full night of sleep and after 24 hours of sleep deprivation. Sleep-deprived subjects showed:
- **Increased preference for high-risk / high-reward options** — a shift in the value function toward variance-seeking.
- **Reduced sensitivity to losses** (blunted loss aversion).
- **Attenuated ventromedial PFC and anterior insula activation** in response to loss outcomes — i.e. the neural signal that normally tracks "that hurt, don't do that" was weaker.
- **Unchanged or elevated nucleus accumbens response** to reward — i.e. the approach signal was intact while the avoid signal was blunted.

This is the mechanism: sleep deprivation **biases the reward/loss asymmetry toward the reward side.** For a PM, this predicts more chasing, more doubling-down, less cutting of losing positions. It is the neural substrate for the stereotype of the tired trader who wouldn't stop trading — and the data are cleaner than the stereotype.

**Whitney, Hinson, Jackson, Van Dongen 2015 — feedback learning.** Whitney et al. ([2015, *Sleep* 38:745–754](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25515105/)) used a reversal-learning task in which feedback contingencies change mid-task. Sleep-deprived subjects learned the initial contingency normally but **failed to update when contingencies reversed** — they kept acting on the old rule. In market terms: regime change is exactly what sleep-deprived cognition fails to detect. This is a direct claim about why a tired PM will keep running the pre-inflection playbook longer than a rested one.

**Maric et al. 2017 — metacognitive accuracy.** Maric, Lustenberger, Werth et al. ([2017, *Sleep* 40:zsx160](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28938801/)) showed that sleep deprivation degrades **metacognitive accuracy** — the correlation between confidence and correctness — while leaving first-order performance relatively intact. This is the cross-reference to file 01 (metacognition): sleep-deprived subjects are not only worse at the task, they are *worse at knowing they are worse*. Subjective competence outruns objective competence. For a PM this is the miscalibration signature that shows up in journaled conviction levels versus realized hit rates.

**Chronic partial sleep restriction — Van Dongen et al. 2003.** Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington, Dinges ([2003, *Sleep* 26:117–126](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/)) is the benchmark chronic-sleep-restriction study. Subjects restricted to 4h or 6h in bed for 14 consecutive nights showed **cumulative, dose-dependent deficits in psychomotor vigilance and working memory equivalent to 1–2 nights of total sleep deprivation by the end of the two weeks** — and, critically, **subjective sleepiness plateaued after a few days while objective performance continued to degrade.** People who chronically sleep 6 hours are functionally impaired and do not know it. The implication for a finance culture that normalizes 5–6 hour sleep is severe: the desk is running at a performance level equivalent to a one-night all-nighter, permanently, while reporting feeling "fine."

## Market-level evidence — DST anomaly, SAD, FOMC timing

**The daylight-saving anomaly — Kamstra, Kramer, Levi 2000.** [Kamstra, Kramer, Levi (2000, *American Economic Review* 90:1005–1011, "Losing Sleep at the Market: The Daylight Saving Anomaly")](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.90.4.1005) examined equity returns on the Monday after the spring-forward and fall-back DST transitions in the US, UK, Canada, and Germany. They report that **the average return on the DST-following Monday is roughly 2–5× more negative than an average Monday**, with the spring-forward (when an hour of sleep is lost) and fall-back (when sleep routines disrupt) both showing the effect. They frame this as a one-hour-scale natural experiment on sleep disruption's market impact.

**Honest caveat.** The result has been challenged. Pinegar ([2002, *American Economic Review* 92:1256–1263](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/00028280260344768)) showed the effect is sensitive to outlier weekends and the choice of sample window. Berument, Dogan, Onar ([2010, *J Econ & Fin*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-008-9065-z)) and others find the anomaly attenuates or disappears in extended samples. The effect should be presented as *suggestive of a population-level sleep effect on collective financial decision-making, with caveats* — not as a traded anomaly. For the RQS audience the value is in the *mechanism claim* (collective sleep loss → collective risk-aversion shift), not in an alpha source.

**SAD and the winter-blues cycle — Kamstra, Kramer, Levi 2003.** Kamstra, Kramer, Levi ([2003, *American Economic Review* 93:324–343, "Winter Blues: A SAD Stock Market Cycle"](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/000282803321455322)) document that equity returns covary with the annual SAD cycle — low returns in autumn as daylight contracts, higher returns as daylight expands. The proposed mechanism is seasonal affective disorder–linked risk aversion, which in turn is driven by seasonal melatonin and serotonin shifts tied to photoperiod. Replications exist ([Garrett, Kamstra, Kramer 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2257.2005.00303.x)) and critiques exist ([Jacobsen & Marquering 2008, *J Banking & Finance* 32:526–540](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2007.06.012) — "Is it the weather?"). Again: mechanism-suggestive, not alpha-grade.

**FOMC and announcement-timing effects.** The broader literature on announcement-day patterns is not primarily a chronobiology story but intersects with it. Lucca & Moench ([2015, *Journal of Finance* 70:329–371](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.12196)) document the **pre-FOMC announcement drift** — large, persistent positive equity returns in the 24 hours before scheduled FOMC announcements. The chronobiology angle: FOMC day itself is a sustained high-attention, high-arousal event, and cortisol responses at pre-FOMC have been documented in both floor traders (Coates & Herbert, 2008) and institutional desk staff. The timing — announcement 14:00 ET — means the high-impact period lands in the post-lunch circadian dip (14:00–16:00) for ET-based traders, a known trough in vigilance (Roach et al. 2006). Desks that respond to FOMC prints within minutes are doing so at one of the lowest-alertness points of the trading day.

Boguth, Grégoire, Martineau ([2019, *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* 54:2327–2363](https://doi.org/10.1017/S002210901800103X)) and related work on scheduled-announcement behavior document that analyst forecast dispersion, intraday volatility, and order-flow patterns all cluster around scheduled macro events in ways that are *consistent* with acute arousal-driven attention narrowing — which dovetails with the stress and sleep literature.

**Lucey and Dowling — sentiment and seasonality review.** Lucey & Dowling ([2005, *Journal of Economic Surveys* 19:211–237, "The Role of Feelings in Investor Decision-Making"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.0950-0804.2005.00245.x)) is the standard review of mood-and-market anomalies — weather, biorhythms, sports outcomes, sunshine. It is methodologically honest about the multiple-comparisons problem in the cross-sectional anomalies literature. For RQS: cite as the careful-framing paper.

**Multiple-comparisons caveat.** Harvey, Liu, Zhu ([2016, *Rev Financial Studies* 29:5–68](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhv059)) show that the cross-section-of-returns literature is dense with statistical artefacts; the same critique applies to the mood / sleep / seasonality anomalies. The right posture for the RQS talk: **use DST and SAD to make the mechanism point, not the alpha point.** If population-scale sleep loss shifts collective risk preferences, the market will price that in quickly and we are not proposing you trade it — we are proposing you do not *fall victim* to it in your own behavior.

## Jet lag, cross-time-zone desks, and chronotype mismatch

**The NBA / NHL natural experiment.** Sports science has run the cleanest jet-lag experiments available, because schedules are fixed and performance is measured objectively every game. Song et al. ([2017, *Sleep* 40:zsw036](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28364419/)) analyzed 11 NBA seasons and showed that teams traveling eastward (compressing their circadian day — analogous to "spring forward") lost home advantage; westward travel was neutral. Roy & Forest ([2018, *J Sleep Res*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29265568/)) documented similar directionality effects in the NHL. The mechanism — circadian misalignment between internal clock and local demand — is identical to what a London-based trader flying to New York for a Tuesday pitch is doing.

**Cross-time-zone trading desks as a natural experiment.** There is almost no published work on this, but two observations are available from the human-factors literature. First, shift-work studies — Åkerstedt & Wright ([2009, *Sleep Med Clin* 4:257–271](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20640236/)) — show that night-shift workers never fully phase-adapt under real-world lighting conditions, so the "I've been on this schedule a while, I'm fine" self-report is unreliable. Second, call-outs, errors, and litigation clustering on shift-work studies (Folkard & Lombardi 2006, *Am J Industrial Med*) suggest that handover windows — shift-change — are error-rich. For a 24-hour FX desk with London/NY/HK handovers, the handover windows are the operational-risk equivalent of aviation's takeoff/landing — plausibly the highest-risk minutes of the day.

**Chronotype assessment.** The Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (Horne & Östberg 1976) and the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire (Roenneberg et al. 2003) are validated, 5-minute self-report instruments. Chronotype is ~40–50% heritable ([Kalmbach et al. 2017, *Sleep* 40:zsw048](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28364473/)) and stable in adulthood; it is a *trait*, not a habit, and mismatching a trait-evening person to an early-morning decision window is equivalent to running the desk in a permanent state of mild jet lag. Social jet lag — the gap between weekday and weekend sleep midpoints — is a validated chronotype-mismatch biomarker (Wittmann et al. 2006) and predicts metabolic and cognitive outcomes.

## Sleep and emotional memory consolidation

**REM sleep selectively consolidates emotional memory.** Wagner, Gais, Born ([2001, *Learning & Memory* 8:112–119](http://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/8/2/112)) showed that subjects who slept during the second half of the night (REM-rich) retained emotional memory content specifically better than those who slept the first half (slow-wave-dominant) or did not sleep. Walker & van der Helm ([2009, *Psychol Bull* 135:731–748](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19702380/)) review the broader literature: REM sleep preferentially consolidates the *emotional* content of memory while slow-wave sleep consolidates declarative content.

**Implication: a bad trading day sleeps itself into a trait.** Van der Helm, Yao, Dutt et al. ([2011, *Current Biology* 21:2029–2032](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822%2811%2901198-2)) showed that REM sleep *reduces* the affective tone ("next-day emotional reactivity") of a memory while *preserving* the factual content. The intuition: "sleep on it and it will look different in the morning" has a mechanistic basis — REM strips the charge while retaining the content. Bad night of sleep, or chronic REM suppression (alcohol, late caffeine, benzodiazepines, some antidepressants), defeats this process. **A drawdown you sleep poorly on becomes more emotionally encoded than one you sleep well on**, and emotional encoding feeds loss-aversion and avoidance behavior on the next analogous trade.

**REM and creative problem-solving — Wagner, Gais, Haider, Verleger, Born 2004.** Wagner et al. ([2004, *Nature* 427:352–355](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature02223)) gave subjects a Number Reduction Task with a hidden shortcut rule. After 8 hours' sleep, twice as many subjects discovered the rule as after equivalent wake. Cai, Mednick, Harrison, Kanady, Mednick ([2009, *PNAS* 106:10130–10134](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0900271106)) showed that REM-containing naps enhanced performance on the Remote Associates Test — a standard creativity measure — while non-REM naps did not. **For portfolio thinking, which is heavily cross-domain pattern-recognition, REM-preserving sleep is the work.** It is not a recovery phase for the real job; it is part of the real job.

## Practical fund-level interventions

The economics here are unusually favorable. Sleep interventions are cheap, behavioral, and draw on a domain where the primary literature is unambiguous — so the cost-benefit math is strong even if the *marginal* benefit in any given case is modest.

**1. Sleep-policy and culture signalling.** The Brigham Women's Hospital ICU intervention and analogous medical-resident work (Landrigan et al. 2004, *NEJM* 351:1838 — the landmark resident-hours study) demonstrate that capping shift duration reduces serious medical errors by 36% and diagnostic errors by 5.6×. The analogous move in finance is leadership explicitly de-normalizing short sleep and publicly valuing it. Goldman and other banks have moved on junior-analyst hours (the 80-hour soft cap after 2014) — the result is worth reading on its own.

**2. Scheduling around high-stakes decisions.**
- **Do not approve major reallocations in the first 60 minutes after a cross-time-zone flight landing.** This is a free, signable HR/compliance rule.
- **Do not hold risk-committee meetings in the 02:00–05:00 window** — applies to Asia-hour desks with Americas-based risk approval.
- **Schedule FOMC post-mortem decisions the morning after, not in the post-announcement window.** The post-FOMC hour is simultaneously high-arousal and circadian-trough — empirically the worst decision environment of the day.
- **Pre-CPI, pre-NFP: protect the previous night's sleep.** A desk policy of "no non-emergent late-night work the night before a scheduled macro event" is a very cheap risk management intervention.

**3. Wearable HRV / sleep tracking.** Cross-reference the network-neuroscience agent. Consumer-grade wearables (Oura, Whoop, Apple Watch) provide night-by-night total sleep time, sleep efficiency, and HRV — the last of which is a validated proxy for autonomic/PFC state (see file 04 on fight/flight and parasympathetic tone). The actionable use at a desk: **a simple "green/amber/red" self-check before size-changing decisions, where "red" = <6h sleep or HRV >1 SD below personal baseline, and the rule is "execute the existing playbook, don't originate new risk."** This is not invasive, does not require the firm to see individual data, and directly operationalizes the Killgore finding that subjective sleepiness and objective impairment decouple.

**4. Behavioral guardrails.**
- **Pre-decision checklist item:** "When did I last sleep ≥7 hours?" — explicitly listed on the same checklist as position-sizing criteria (see debiasing file 06).
- **Journaled conviction ratings** adjusted by a sleep-quality covariate. This is cheap and gives a within-person calibration signal over time.
- **"Sleep on it" rule for non-time-critical allocations.** Formalize it: an allocation of >X bps of risk that is not time-critical must be slept on. The rule encodes the REM-consolidation and amygdala-cooling literature directly.

**5. Light, caffeine, and environmental hygiene.**
- Morning bright-light exposure (>10,000 lux for 15–30 minutes, typical of outdoor daylight even on cloudy days — an overcast New York morning is still ~5,000–10,000 lux) is the single most effective phase-anchoring intervention ([Wright et al. 2013, *Current Biology* 23:1554–1558](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822%2813%2900764-1)).
- Evening blue-light restriction (amber-tinted screens, dimmed ambient after 21:00) advances melatonin onset modestly ([Chang et al. 2015, *PNAS* 112:1232–1237](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1418490112)).
- Temperature: bedroom 17–19°C is the modal sleep-research optimum; above 24°C objectively degrades sleep architecture.

**ROI framing.** A ~60% amplification of amygdala reactivity (Yoo) and a 14-day half-life decay into a ~1-night-all-nighter-equivalent performance at 6h/night (Van Dongen) together imply that, in a firm whose P&L sensitivity to a single decision is in the low-single-digit basis points, a sleep policy that moves the desk from an average 6h to an average 7h is competitive with many explicit process improvements on a cost-adjusted basis. The honest caveat is that this is an upper-bound argument — direct causal trading-P&L evidence is thin. The lower bound is still positive: sleep is *uncorrelated* with essentially every other lever in the book (hiring, research budget, risk system, factor exposure), so the intervention has no crowding-out cost.

## Caffeine and pharmacology — what helps, what doesn't

**Caffeine.** Well-characterized adenosine antagonist; elevates vigilance and psychomotor reaction time. Caffeine **restores vigilance but does not restore complex executive function.** Wesensten, Belenky, Thorne et al. ([2002, *J Sleep Res* 11:159–167](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12028481/)) showed that 600 mg caffeine during 54-hour sleep deprivation restored simple reaction time to baseline but did *not* restore Tower of London planning performance. The implication for PMs: a morning coffee will keep you awake in the meeting; it will not restore the capacity to make the decision the meeting is about.

- Half-life ~5 hours (highly variable; 3–9 hours across CYP1A2 genotypes). **Afternoon caffeine demonstrably degrades next-night sleep** even when subjects report no difficulty falling asleep ([Drake et al. 2013, *J Clin Sleep Med* 9:1195–1200](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24235903/): 400 mg of caffeine 0, 3, or 6 hours before bed all reduced total sleep time — the 6-hour-before condition by more than one hour).
- Risk for PMs: the "I need coffee to get through the afternoon" reflex is a self-reinforcing cycle — afternoon caffeine degrades nighttime sleep, nighttime sleep loss creates afternoon fatigue, afternoon fatigue drives caffeine use. Breaking this loop is one of the cheapest single interventions.

**Modafinil and stimulants.** Modafinil (a wakefulness-promoting agent used clinically for narcolepsy and shift-work sleep disorder) has been tested in military sleep-deprivation settings. Baranski et al. ([2004, *J Sleep Res* 13:143–159](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15175093/)) found modafinil improved vigilance during sleep deprivation but, like caffeine, **did not restore higher-order executive function and produced subjective overconfidence in performance** — i.e. the same miscalibration signature as sleep deprivation itself. There is no published evidence of modafinil use improving trading outcomes, and the overconfidence finding is specifically concerning in a context (portfolio decisions) where miscalibration is the bias we are most trying to suppress.

**Alcohol.** Acute alcohol impairs prefrontal-dependent functions in a dose-dependent manner and — more relevant for the "morning after" discussion — **suppresses REM sleep in the first half of the night and produces REM rebound in the second half with fragmented sleep architecture** ([Ebrahim et al. 2013, *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research* 37:539–549](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)). The next-day effect on judgment is well-documented in driving and surgery (Howe et al. 2018, *J Surg Res*; Williamson et al. 2000, *Occup Environ Med* 57:649–655 showing that 17-hour wake + moderate alcohol is equivalent to legal DWI-level impairment). For PMs: a client-dinner decision environment systematically runs below the level of a 3AM call, and the "morning after" is worse than it feels because REM-disruption degrades exactly the emotional-memory-consolidation and creative-integration process described above.

**Melatonin.** Effective as a *chronobiotic* (shifting phase) but mediocre as a sleep aid. Low-dose (0.3–0.5 mg) melatonin 3–5 hours before desired sleep onset advances the circadian phase by ~30–45 minutes per night — useful for east-bound travel and shift adaptation. Higher doses (3–10 mg, the consumer dosing) provide little additional benefit and saturate melatonin receptors ([Burgess, Revell, Molina, Eastman 2010, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 95:3325–3331](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20410229/)). The operational use at a desk: melatonin is for fixing jet lag, not for sleeping through a bad night.

## Bridge to our empirical findings

The 2022 RQS-analyst horizon-compression finding (see file 03) was framed there as a cortisol/catecholamine phenotype driven by a record bond drawdown. The sleep literature adds a *co-factor* that is, on the available evidence, likely to have amplified the same signal.

**Five reasons to think 2022 was a sleep-amplified stress event.**
1. **Duration.** The 2022 rates selloff was a sustained 9-month event (see file 03). Sustained elevated cortisol impairs sleep architecture — elevated evening cortisol delays sleep onset, suppresses slow-wave sleep, and fragments REM (Steiger 2002, *Sleep Med Rev* 6:125–138). The drawdown and the sleep disruption are mutually reinforcing.
2. **Late-night news cycle.** 2022 had unusual volumes of overnight macro news — geopolitical (Russia invasion, Feb 24 2022), central-bank off-schedule commentary, CPI surprises. Rates analysts whose coverage requires monitoring the Asia-session open routinely surrender the first block of sleep in these regimes.
3. **The Van Dongen dose-response implies a plausible attribution share.** If the desk's average sleep fell from ~7h to ~6h over the year, the performance impact at day-14 of that restriction is equivalent to one full sleepless night — i.e. the Yoo-Walker phenotype, permanently. Attribution of the 2022 horizon compression to *sleep specifically* is not cleanly identified in our data, but the literature is consistent with sleep being a non-trivial share.
4. **REM-consolidation amplification.** REM-preserved sleep reduces next-day affective tone of bad-P&L days (Van der Helm 2011). REM-deprived sleep preserves the affective charge. Over a 9-month drawdown, chronic REM deprivation plausibly compounds the affective encoding of losses — producing a phenotype that looks like, and may mechanistically partly be, a progressive shift from state to trait loss-aversion.
5. **Testable in our own data.** If we obtain any time-stamped behavioral proxy — commit-time on written research, first-email time, login-time — we can regress the horizon-compression metric on a sleep-proxy covariate (time-between-last-activity-yesterday and first-activity-today, rough as it is) and see whether sleep-short weeks predict more pronounced horizon compression. This is a concrete next analysis.

**Falsifiability.** The chronobiology story is *not* an alternative to the cortisol story — the two mechanisms converge on the same PFC-offline / amygdala-dominant phenotype. The relevant question is not "is it stress or is it sleep?" but "*of the total phenotype we observe, what share is each channel contributing?*" The honest answer given what we have is: both, mutually reinforcing, with cortisol the proximate driver and sleep the amplifier.

**For the RQS audience, this is the closer.** Every other slide in the deck has a remediation that is slow, political, or methodologically contested. Sleep has a remediation that is behavioral, private, policy-compatible, and supported by an unambiguous primary literature. If you remember one intervention from this deck, make it the sleep one.

## Candidate canonical graphs

1. **Yoo 2007 amygdala BOLD response — sleep-deprived vs. rested — on increasingly aversive stimuli.** Two lines, straight segments with visible dots at each aversiveness bin. No curves. The headline: ~60% amplification, visible at a glance. The single most important slide in the section.

2. **Yoo 2007 amygdala–mPFC functional connectivity — sleep-deprived vs. rested.** Two-bar chart of the PPI regression coefficient. Rested bar positive (mPFC inhibits amygdala), sleep-deprived bar near zero or negative. The "disconnect" claim made visible.

3. **Diurnal cortisol curve, with the CAR highlighted.** One day of clock hours on the x-axis, salivary cortisol (nmol/L) on the y-axis. Markers at +0, +15, +30, +45, +60 min post-wake. Overlay a US cash-equity session band (09:30–16:00 ET) as a shaded region. This slide makes the point that the trading day begins at the circadian cortisol peak.

4. **Van Dongen 2003 chronic-sleep-restriction dose-response.** Psychomotor vigilance task lapses over 14 days, three conditions: 8h, 6h, 4h in bed. Three straight lines with visible daily dots. The headline: 6h and 4h lines diverge from 8h early and continue to degrade; **subjective sleepiness plateaus** (a second panel showing self-rated sleepiness flattening after ~day 3 while objective lapses continue to climb).

5. **Kamstra DST anomaly — Monday returns after spring-forward vs. normal Mondays, four markets.** Four pairs of bars (US, UK, CA, DE), showing average DST-Monday return vs. average Monday return. Note the caveat in the caption (Pinegar 2002 — robust only in some samples). Honest slide, not an alpha pitch.

6. **Venkatraman 2007 risky-choice asymmetry.** Two bars: rested vs. sleep-deprived share of high-risk-high-reward choices for gains; two bars for losses. Rested subjects show reflection-effect pattern; sleep-deprived subjects show amplified pattern. Ideally re-plotted from the published data with a second small-multiple of the vmPFC/insula BOLD attenuation to loss feedback.

7. **The integrated mechanism diagram.** One slide combining file 03 (stress → cortisol/catecholamine → PFC offline → amygdala dominant → horizon compression) with sleep as a **parallel amplifier pathway** (sleep loss → PFC offline → amygdala dominant). Two arrows converging on the same downstream node. This is the integration slide that makes sleep not a side-topic but a co-mechanism of the main finding.

8. **The intervention grid.** A 2×2 matrix: x = cost, y = leverage. Sleep policy and pre-decision sleep checks sit in low-cost / high-leverage. Wearable HRV programs sit in moderate-cost / high-leverage. Formal resident-hours-style scheduling rules sit in moderate-cost / moderate-leverage. This is the "pick one thing and do it" slide at the end of the section.
