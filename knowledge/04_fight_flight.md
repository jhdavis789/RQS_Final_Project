# Fight vs. Flight — Deep Research

## Executive one-liner
Under acute stress, catecholamine and cortisol surges pull the prefrontal cortex offline and hand control to the amygdala, striatum, and periaqueductal gray — so whether a portfolio manager *fights* (active risk up, bet harder) or *flees* (active risk down, hug the benchmark) in a crisis is not just a preference but the behavioral read-out of a specific neurocircuit, and the data should tell us which one is the more error-prone under our conditions.

---

## The four responses

The classical "fight-or-flight" binary, coined by Walter Cannon in *Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage* (1915), has been extended by a century of work into at least four distinguishable acute-threat responses. They are not a menu the organism chooses from deliberately; they are reflexive states selected by the nervous system based on threat proximity, escapability, controllability, sex, and individual trait profile.

**1. Fight.** Active, aggressive, approach-oriented defense. Behavior: attack, confrontation, conviction-laden action. Physiology: sympathetic discharge, norepinephrine and epinephrine surge, elevated testosterone if baseline cortisol is low, hypertension, tachycardia, non-opioid analgesia (Bandler & Shipley, 1994). Neural locus: medial amygdala → dorsomedial hypothalamus → dorsolateral and lateral columns of the periaqueductal gray (dlPAG/lPAG). Panksepp's "RAGE" circuit ([Panksepp, 1998, 2011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763411001497)) anatomically overlaps with the dorsal-PAG fight pathway.

**2. Flight.** Active escape. Behavior: withdrawal, risk reduction, disengagement. Physiology: similar autonomic profile to fight (sympathetic dominance), but motor output is avoidance rather than approach. Neural locus: dlPAG drives flight under imminent proximal threat ([Mobbs et al., 2007](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1144298); [Mobbs et al., 2009](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/29/39/12236)).

**3. Freeze.** Immobility under threat. Behavior: paralysis, non-action, attention locked but motor output suppressed. Two distinct subtypes:
- *Attentive freeze* (a.k.a. "freezing for action") — active vigilance with suppressed movement, sympathetically elevated, still poised to fight or flee; dlPAG-mediated ([Roelofs, 2017](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstb/article/372/1718/20160206/23227/Freeze-for-action-neurobiological-mechanisms-in)).
- *Tonic immobility / passive coping* — parasympathetic shutdown, bradycardia, hypotension, opioid-mediated analgesia; ventrolateral PAG (vlPAG) and, in Porges' framework, dorsal vagal complex ([Porges, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17049418/); [Schauer & Elbert, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21137776/); [Kozlowska et al., 2015](https://journals.lww.com/hrpjournal/fulltext/2015/07000/fear_and_the_defense_cascade__clinical.3.aspx)). This is the "defense cascade" endpoint when fight and flight are both unavailable.

**4. Tend-and-befriend.** Proposed by Shelley Taylor and colleagues as an alternative female-biased stress response ([Taylor et al., 2000, *Psychological Review*](https://taylorlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2014/10/2000_Biobehavioral-responses-to-stress-in-females_tend-and-befriend.pdf)). Behavior: affiliative, caregiving, social-network-building. Physiology: oxytocin-mediated, dampens HPA-axis cortisol response, lowers heart rate and blood pressure. Neural locus: hypothalamic oxytocinergic projections + amygdala modulation + ventral vagal complex (Porges). The key empirical observation is that the same stressor can yield sympathetic-dominant fight/flight in some individuals and oxytocin-dominant affiliative coping in others.

The relevance for PMs: all four are latent in a decision-maker at the moment of a market dislocation. The variable isn't whether the autonomic nervous system responds — it does, in seconds — it's which *program* gets selected.

## Brain regions: a guided tour

For the RQS audience, seven structures matter. The descriptions below are keyed to the single circuit diagram we should show on one slide.

**1. Amygdala.** Fast-lane fear detector. Receives thalamic shortcut inputs (LeDoux's "low road") that bypass cortex. Under acute stress the amygdala is *potentiated* — its connectivity with prefrontal regulatory regions weakens while its connectivity with brainstem output regions strengthens ([Arnsten, 2009](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2648); [Arnsten, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4816215/)). In Panksepp's taxonomy this is the anchor of the FEAR system.

**2. Periaqueductal gray (PAG).** Midbrain structure organized as four longitudinal columns — dorsomedial (dmPAG), dorsolateral (dlPAG), lateral (lPAG), and ventrolateral (vlPAG) — each of which drives a different defensive program ([Bandler & Shipley, 1994, *TINS*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7817403/)). Activation mapping:
- dmPAG / dlPAG: active defense — fight, flight, tachycardia, hypertension, non-opioid analgesia.
- lPAG: defensive attack, confrontation.
- vlPAG: passive coping — quiescence, hyporeactivity, bradycardia, hypotension, opioid-mediated analgesia (the freeze/collapse endpoint).

The PAG is the *output* node where defensive programs are physically coordinated. Higher-order regions (amygdala, hypothalamus, PFC) project *onto* PAG columns; PAG columns project *out* to autonomic and motor centers.

**3. Prefrontal cortex (PFC).** Top-down regulator of thought, attention, and emotion — dorsolateral regions (dlPFC) for cognition, ventromedial regions (vmPFC) for emotion regulation ([Arnsten, 2009](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2648)). This is the region most sensitive to stress-induced impairment. The PFC normally *suppresses* amygdala and subcortical defense circuits; under acute stress this suppression fails. The PFC is also the seat of cognitive appraisal — the "is this actually dangerous?" process that distal threats engage but proximal threats bypass ([Mobbs et al., 2007](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1144298)).

**4. Striatum.** Habit and reward. Under acute stress, control shifts from dorsomedial-striatum/PFC-based goal-directed action to dorsolateral-striatum-based habit ([Schwabe & Wolf, 2009, *J Neurosci*](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/29/22/7191); [Schwabe & Wolf, 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0166432811000258)). What we call "reverting to training" or "muscle memory" in stressed professionals is the striatum taking over from the PFC.

**5. Locus coeruleus (LC).** Brainstem nucleus, sole source of cortical norepinephrine. Two modes:
- *Phasic:* task-related bursts, support exploitation and focus.
- *Tonic:* sustained elevated firing, support exploration, distractibility, and attentional broadening outside the current task ([Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005, *Annual Review of Neuroscience*](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.neuro.28.061604.135709)).

Under acute threat the LC shifts to tonic-high firing, flooding cortex with norepinephrine — this is a large part of how the PFC is knocked offline ([Arnsten, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4816215/)).

**6. Hypothalamus.** Command center for both axes — sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) via descending sympathetic pathways and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) via corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) → pituitary → adrenal cortex → cortisol.

**7. Adrenal glands.** Medulla releases epinephrine and norepinephrine (SAM axis, seconds). Cortex releases cortisol (HPA axis, minutes-to-hours).

## Fast vs. slow: SAM vs. HPA axes

Two parallel stress systems, two timescales. They are not redundant — they do different things on different clocks, and the decision-making consequences differ accordingly.

**SAM — Sympathetic Adrenal Medullary axis (seconds).** Hypothalamus → preganglionic sympathetic neurons → adrenal medulla → epinephrine and norepinephrine release. Also direct noradrenergic projections from the locus coeruleus to cortex. Effects are autonomic and near-instant: elevated heart rate, blood pressure, pupil dilation, bronchodilation, glycogenolysis, peripheral vasoconstriction. Cognitively, the LC-NE surge biases attention toward salient stimuli, narrows it (tunneling), and strengthens bottom-up over top-down control ([Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.neuro.28.061604.135709); [Arnsten, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4816215/)).

**HPA — Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal axis (minutes to hours).** Paraventricular hypothalamus releases CRH → anterior pituitary releases ACTH → adrenal cortex releases glucocorticoids, primarily cortisol in humans. Cortisol peaks roughly 20–40 minutes after stressor onset. Effects are genomic and metabolic: glucose mobilization, immune suppression, broad modulation of hippocampal/PFC/amygdala function.

**Decision-making implications within a crisis window:**
- *First 10 seconds to first minute:* SAM dominates. Pure autonomic arousal, pupil dilation, attention narrowing. Fight vs. flight selection is effectively already locked in by the end of this window based on threat proximity (Mobbs switch — see below).
- *First 10 minutes:* SAM still elevated, HPA rising. Cortisol begins to bind glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid receptors in PFC and hippocampus. Working memory degrades. Goal-directed → habitual shift begins ([Schwabe & Wolf, 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0166432811000258)).
- *30 minutes to several hours:* Cortisol at peak. Risk preferences shift substantially. Delay discounting steepens — future payoffs are more heavily discounted ([Kimura et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4888297/); [Haushofer et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3400439/)). Temporal sensitivity decreases. Sooner rewards become more attractive.
- *Hours to days:* If stress persists (chronic), the Kandasamy et al. effect dominates — risk premium collapses by ~44% and probability weighting of small events becomes more exaggerated ([Kandasamy et al., 2014, *PNAS*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1317908111)).

For a PM inside a five-minute drawdown, SAM is fully on and HPA is spooling. For a PM inside a five-week drawdown, both are elevated and cortisol is driving chronic risk aversion. The "fight/flight" label is the same in both windows but the underlying chemistry and the decision-making distortion are not.

## PFC offline — the Arnsten thesis

This is the single most important mechanism for our story. It is also the one with the most quantitative experimental backing.

Amy Arnsten's program, summarized most clearly in her two *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* papers ([Arnsten, 2009](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2648); [Arnsten, 2015, "Stress weakens prefrontal networks"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4816215/)), makes the following cumulative argument:

1. **PFC is uniquely sensitive to catecholamine dose.** Performance follows an inverted-U curve: too little norepinephrine and dopamine, PFC underperforms; too much (i.e., during acute uncontrollable stress), PFC underperforms again. Only a narrow middle zone gives top-down regulation.

2. **The mechanism is intracellular and specific.** Under high catecholamine drive, α1-adrenoceptor and D1-receptor stimulation activates feedforward calcium-protein kinase C (PKC) and cyclic-AMP-PKA signaling in layer III dlPFC spines. This opens potassium (HCN and KCNQ) channels and *weakens* synaptic efficacy at recurrent network synapses — precisely the ones that sustain working-memory representations.

3. **Effect is rapid and reversible.** Within minutes of stressor onset, PFC-dependent cognition degrades. Within minutes of stressor offset (if cortisol hasn't accumulated enough to produce lasting changes), it recovers.

4. **The shift is not loss of function — it is *reallocation*.** While PFC goes offline, the same catecholamine surge *strengthens* amygdala-dependent emotional learning and striatal habit control. The brain doesn't get dumber in the aggregate; it stops being reflective and starts being reflexive. The Arnsten 2015 phrasing: "high levels of catecholamines take the PFC off-line while strengthening the functions of more primitive circuits — for example, the conditioned emotional responses of the amygdala and the habitual actions of the basal ganglia."

5. **Chronic stress makes this structural.** Repeated exposure produces dendritic retraction in layer III dlPFC pyramidal cells ([Radley et al., 2004, 2006](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2648)) and amygdalar dendritic expansion. The circuit reweights permanently in the direction of emotional reactivity.

**Why this matters for PMs:** A portfolio manager making a decision inside a VIX-50 tape is not the same decision-maker as the one making a decision inside a VIX-15 tape. Not metaphorically — literally. The working-memory capacity available to weigh multiple scenarios is measurably smaller, the tendency to default to trained habits is measurably larger, and the capacity for cognitive reappraisal ("is this actually different this time?") is measurably impaired. The Arnsten thesis is the biological substrate of why "I'll just follow my process" is easier said than done in a drawdown.

## Proximal vs. distal threat — the Mobbs switch

[Mobbs et al. (2007), *Science*, "When fear is near: threat imminence elicits prefrontal-periaqueductal gray shifts in humans"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1144298) is the citation that makes the fight/flight frame concrete and fMRI-measurable.

**Paradigm.** Volunteers played a virtual-pursuit game inside an MRI scanner. A "predator" (a dot on a 2D maze) pursued the subject's avatar. When caught, the subject received a painful electric shock. Predator-distance was parametrically varied trial by trial.

**Result.** As predator distance shrank, BOLD activity shifted systematically:
- Distal threat ("postencounter" phase, predator far): activity concentrated in ventromedial PFC, subgenual ACC, hippocampus, amygdala — cognitive-evaluative regions.
- Proximal threat ("circa-strike" phase, predator near): activity shifted abruptly to mid-dorsal ACC and midbrain PAG — reflexive defense regions.
- The shift scaled with anticipated pain magnitude.
- PAG activity at the proximal phase correlated with subjective dread and *decreased* confidence of escape.

The 2009 follow-up ([Mobbs et al., 2009, *J Neurosci*](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/29/39/12236)) replicated and extended this: locomotor errors during active avoidance correlated with midbrain activity, consistent with hardwired midbrain defense taking over when PFC has been bypassed.

**Translation.** The brain operates two defense modes and switches between them based on whether threat is evaluable or immediate. Distal = "I have time to think; PFC-led cognitive appraisal." Proximal = "No time; midbrain reflex." The switch is not a deliberate strategic choice. It happens fast, outside conscious control, and the thresholds are set by individual history and current autonomic state.

**Application to PMs.** A slow-rolling drawdown (3% over a month) is processed by distal-threat circuitry. The PM reads research, talks to analysts, runs scenarios, executes changes deliberately. A gap-down 8% in 90 minutes on a single Fed headline is processed by proximal-threat circuitry. Whatever the PM does in that window is likely to be executed with midbrain defense circuits heavily engaged and PFC deliberation suppressed. Whether the resulting output is *fight* (double down) or *flight* (de-risk to benchmark) depends on pre-existing dispositional biases, baseline hormones, and the specific PAG column activated. But it is *not* the same cognitive system that produced the slower decision.

This is the mechanistic frame for why the empirical finding — *are stress-window PM trades better or worse than calm-window PM trades?* — is diagnostic. The stress-window trade is the output of a different neural circuit. If that circuit is systematically better at this particular task (e.g., because it overweights hard-earned heuristics that calm-window decisions dilute), the stressed PM wins. If it is systematically worse (e.g., because it substitutes habit for analysis on a task where the habit is miscalibrated), the stressed PM loses.

## The trading-floor bridge

The Arnsten and Mobbs work above is *in vitro* (animals, MRI volunteers). [Coates & Herbert (2008), *PNAS*, "Endogenous steroids and financial risk taking on a London trading floor"](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.0704025105) is the closest the literature gets to *in vivo* measurement of these systems in actual professional investors under actual market stress.

**Methodology.**
- N = 17 male City of London traders.
- 8 consecutive working days.
- Saliva samples twice daily: 11:00 (before lunch) and 16:00 (end of afternoon session).
- Steroid assays for testosterone and cortisol.
- P&L tracked per trader per day.
- Market volatility (implied and realized) tracked in parallel.

**Findings.**
1. **Morning testosterone predicted daily P&L.** On days when a trader's 11:00 testosterone was above his individual median, his day's P&L exceeded his median too. Fourteen of seventeen traders showed this pattern.
2. **Cortisol tracked volatility, not P&L.** A trader's cortisol rose with the variance of his own trading results *and* with market volatility (implied volatility of bond futures). On one representative trader, when implied volatility rose from a baseline of ~0.5% to a stressed ~0.8% over a week, afternoon cortisol rose by ~68%.
3. **The interpretation in Coates & Herbert's own words:** "Higher testosterone may contribute to economic return, whereas cortisol is increased by risk." And: "If the acutely elevated steroids observed were to persist or increase as volatility rises, they may shift risk preferences and even affect a trader's ability to engage in rational choice."

**Caveats (we must note these).**
- N=17, all male, one trading desk, high-frequency rates/futures. Generalization to asset-allocation PMs is by analogy, not by replication.
- Correlational. Morning testosterone predicting P&L could mean hormones drive performance, or anticipation of a good day drives hormones, or both.
- Winner effect proposed as a causal mechanism but not directly tested in this paper.

**The follow-up that closes the causal gap.** [Kandasamy et al. (2014), *PNAS*, "Cortisol shifts financial risk preferences"](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1317908111). Volunteers (N=36, both sexes) were dosed with hydrocortisone over 8 days using regimens designed to raise circulating cortisol to levels Coates & Herbert had observed in traders during high-volatility weeks. Risk preferences were measured before and after via standard incentive-compatible elicitations.

- Acute cortisol spikes had little behavioral effect.
- *Chronic* elevated cortisol (8-day dosing) reduced willingness to take risk substantially. The best-fit utility risk premium fell by ~44% in the high-cortisol condition.
- Probability weighting of small-probability events became more distorted (overweighting), particularly in men.

This is the causal loop Coates argued for in *The Hour Between Dog and Wolf* (2012):
- Rising volatility → rising cortisol in market participants.
- Persistent elevated cortisol → reduced risk appetite across participants.
- Aggregated reduced risk appetite → selling pressure, widening spreads, liquidity withdrawal.
- Which is itself observed by remaining participants as further rising volatility.
- Feedback loop. A physiological mechanism that contributes to market overshoots on the downside.

On the upside, Coates proposes the parallel "winner effect": sustained wins elevate testosterone, which increases risk-taking, which (in a rising tape) produces more wins, which further elevates testosterone — until the feedback loop ends in overconfidence and a blowup.

**For our deck:** Coates & Herbert is *the* empirical bridge between the neuroscience literature and the behavior of professional risk-takers. It is the single strongest lay-accessible citation we can put in front of the RQS audience.

## How each response modifies decision-making / logic

Mapping each response to the specific neural circuit and the decision-making signature it should produce.

**Fight (dorsomedial/lateral PAG, testosterone-dominant, low cortisol).**
- Logic mode: high-confidence, narrow-hypothesis, commitment-biased. Reduced consideration of disconfirming evidence.
- Behavioral signature in a PM: larger active bets; more off-benchmark positioning; willingness to sell liquidity rather than buy it; longer holding periods for existing conviction names.
- Empirical predictions: trade count up or flat, average absolute active weight change up, tracking error up.
- Warranted iff the PM's pre-formed thesis actually holds in the new regime. The fight response increases commitment *without* increasing the PM's ability to evaluate whether commitment is justified — because PFC-led reappraisal is degraded in parallel.

**Flight (dorsolateral PAG, sympathetically active, escape-motivated).**
- Logic mode: threat-avoidant, simplification-biased, default-seeking. The goal is to reduce variance of outcomes, not to optimize expected return.
- Behavioral signature: de-risking toward benchmark weights; closing positions that have become loss-makers; trimming tails of the active-weight distribution; shortened decision horizons (cortisol-driven delay discounting — [Kimura et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4888297/)).
- Empirical predictions: trade count up, average absolute active weight change down, tracking error down.
- Warranted iff the PM's active positions were in fact mispriced for the new regime. Flight is rational if the prior was wrong; flight is a mistake if the prior was right and you are crystallizing mark-to-market loss at the worst possible price.

**Freeze — attentive (dorsolateral PAG, sympathetically elevated, motor output suppressed).**
- Logic mode: vigilant, information-gathering, action-inhibited. Waiting for clarity.
- Behavioral signature: reduced trade count with no directional shift in active weights; high "watching" / low "doing."
- This is often conflated with *discipline*. It sometimes is discipline. It is also sometimes the output of a circuit where the motor decision threshold has been raised above what the signal can currently cross.

**Freeze — passive (ventrolateral PAG, parasympathetic surge, dorsal vagal shutdown in Porges' terms).**
- Logic mode: disengaged, shut down, cognitively offline. The extreme tail of the freeze response.
- Behavioral signature: functional paralysis. In a PM context: notes ungenerated, calls unmade, decisions indefinitely deferred.
- Rare in selected high-performing professionals but documented in acute trauma and severe drawdown literature. Most relevant to our story as the *limiting case* that sits beyond "flight" on the same axis.

**Tend-and-befriend (hypothalamic oxytocin, HPA-dampening, ventral vagal / social engagement).**
- Logic mode: consensus-seeking, coordination-biased, committee-following. The goal is to not be alone with the decision.
- Behavioral signature: increased communication, more meetings, more deference to the investment committee or the house view; portfolio drift toward peer-median positioning.
- This response has not been extensively studied in male-dominated institutional investment populations — where it likely shows up less as "tend-and-befriend" in the Taylor sense and more as "herding" in the Scharfstein-Stein (1990) sense. The underlying neurochemistry (oxytocin + reduced cortisol via social support) is the same. The behavioral read-out — move with the herd — is the same. The label differs.

**BIS/BAS disposition (Gray / Carver & White).** Trait-level modifier cutting across all four responses. A PM high in BIS is more likely to flee or freeze under stress. A PM high in BAS is more likely to fight. These traits are measurable ([Carver & White, 1994](https://www.psy.miami.edu/faculty/ccarver/bisbas.html)), stable, and predictive. Relevant because any RQS process redesign should assume that different PMs will default to different responses — "stress protocols" must be disposition-aware.

## Bridge to our empirical finding

The presenter's experiment decomposes PM trades during high-volatility windows into two categories:
- **Active-risk-up trades** — moves that push the portfolio *further from* the benchmark. These are the *fight* signature: commitment expanding, bets getting bolder, PFC-driven thesis override.
- **Active-risk-down trades** — moves that pull the portfolio *closer to* the benchmark. These are the *flight* signature: risk-shedding, escape to the crowd, cortisol-driven risk-premium collapse.
- **No trade (the residual)** — the *freeze* signature: motor output suppressed even though information is being processed.

Each of these has a specific neural and hormonal profile (above). Each has a specific success condition under which the behavior is *rational*. The empirical experiment is diagnostic because it tells us, in this specific population (Capital Group PMs) under these specific conditions (high-volatility windows in our sample), *which response produces better-than-average ex-post returns and which produces worse*.

- If *active-risk-up* trades systematically outperform active-risk-down trades in stress windows, the fight response is the valuable one in this population. The behavioral implication is that PMs who flee in stress are leaving alpha on the table. The process implication is that RQS should build scaffolding that *supports* conviction trades during stress (e.g., position-holding norms, pre-commit devices) rather than forcing de-risking protocols.
- If *active-risk-down* trades systematically outperform active-risk-up trades, the flight response is the valuable one. PMs who fight in stress are reliably overcommitting to priors that the new regime has invalidated. The process implication is that RQS should build stress-window guardrails — cooling-off periods, mandatory second-reader sign-off on active-weight expansions, etc.
- If *no-trade* (freeze) dominates, the signal is that sitting still beats both responses and the process implication is about codifying the "do nothing" default during acute vol.

The three hypotheses are not symmetric in prior probability. The weight of the literature above — Arnsten (PFC offline), Schwabe & Wolf (habitual > goal-directed), Kandasamy (chronic cortisol collapses risk premium), Mobbs (midbrain hijack under proximal threat) — *predicts* that decision quality should be *lower* in stress windows than in calm windows, across either direction. The open empirical question our data answers is *which direction the degradation concentrates in* — does stress produce bad fight trades, bad flight trades, or both? That's the bridge from the science to our deck's punchline.

One additional framing the audience will appreciate: this is not an argument about heroes and cowards. The fight-vs-flight selection is not volitional. Two PMs looking at the same tape will produce different responses because their baseline cortisol is different, their trait BIS/BAS profile is different, their history of winning is different (winner effect → testosterone baseline), and their amygdala-PFC coupling at rest is different. A process that assumes all PMs should respond identically under stress is a process that ignores a large and well-measured literature on individual differences in acute stress response.

## Candidate canonical graphs

Four figures, any of which could anchor this section of the deck. Listed in descending order of punch.

**1. Coates & Herbert cortisol-volatility chart (PNAS 2008, Fig. 3).** Trader-level cortisol on the y-axis vs. implied bond-futures volatility on the x-axis, showing positive slope. The cleanest visual link between market-level stress and participant-level hormone response. Prerequisite: redraw in deck theme colors (no black), straight-line fit with visible dots per day, correlation and N labeled.

**2. Arnsten inverted-U PFC performance curve.** Working-memory or delayed-response task accuracy on the y-axis vs. norepinephrine/dopamine availability on the x-axis, showing the classical inverted-U with stress conditions marked on the right (descending) limb. Either redraw from [Arnsten 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4816215/) or reconstruct from referenced primate data. This is the single image that makes "PFC offline" visually concrete.

**3. PAG functional anatomy — four-column schematic.** Coronal section through midbrain with the four PAG columns labeled and color-coded by behavioral output (fight / flight / active-freeze / passive-freeze), inputs from amygdala and hypothalamus shown as arrows in, outputs to autonomic and motor targets shown as arrows out. Derived from [Bandler & Shipley 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7817403/). Draws eyes because anatomy is unfamiliar to an RQS audience.

**4. Mobbs PFC → PAG transition plot.** BOLD signal in ventromedial PFC and PAG as a function of predator proximity on the x-axis, showing the crossover from PFC-dominant (distal) to PAG-dominant (proximal). Redrawn from Mobbs et al. 2007, Fig. 3. This is the "distal → proximal switch" one-pager. Strong visual metaphor for calm-tape vs. stress-tape PM decision-making.

All four should be redrawn to deck theme (no black ink, straight lines, visible data points), with y-axis labeling and N disclosed. Figures 2 and 4 should live on adjacent slides — together they tell the "when stress is distant, PFC is in charge; when stress is near, midbrain is in charge, and the Arnsten inverted-U tells you the PFC is furthest from its peak precisely when you need it most" story.
